## How it works

In a planographic print, the printing surface is flat. Nothing is carved away or etched below it. The image is held on the surface in one of two ways.

In lithography, it is held by chemistry. A greasy drawing on stone, metal, or plastic attracts oily ink, and the bare areas around it are treated so they hold a film of water. Oil and water repel each other, so when the surface is dampened and rolled with ink, the ink sticks only to the drawing. The surface can be inked and printed again and again. Most lithography today is printed offset: the inked plate prints onto a rubber blanket, and the blanket prints onto the paper. The image reverses twice and comes out the same way round as the drawing, so nothing has to be drawn backward. Martin's lithography demonstrations print metal plates this way. <cite>Martin, pp. 11 and 119–123</cite>

In monotype, the image is simply painted, rolled, or wiped onto a smooth plate and printed once. There is only one full print, and often a fainter second one called a ghost. Gel plate printing is a monotype process.

## A short history

Giovanni Benedetto Castiglione made the first monotypes in the 1640s by painting and wiping ink on smooth copper plates. Alois Senefelder discovered lithography in 1796 while looking for a cheap way to print his plays, and found that limestone drawn on with a greasy crayon could be dampened and inked so only the drawing printed. Lithography became the great commercial process of the nineteenth century, and artists such as Toulouse-Lautrec turned the color litho poster into an art form.

The gelatin hectograph of the 1870s let offices and schools copy pages from a tray of gelatin, an ancestor of today's gel plates. In 1960 June Wayne founded the Tamarind Lithography Workshop in Los Angeles to train master printers when the craft was close to disappearing in the United States, which set off a broad revival of artist printmaking.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Monotype is open to every age. A preschooler can finger-paint on a sheet of plexiglass and press paper onto it. Gel plates suit students from the early grades up.

Lithography on stone or aluminum belongs in a college or adult studio. Its traditional chemistry includes nitric acid, solvents, and in older books phenol, gasoline, and dichromate compounds that should not be used. Newer approaches change that picture. Polyester plates drawn with a ballpoint pen or a laser printer can be printed with a wooden spoon and suit high school classes. Kitchen lithography on aluminum foil uses cola and vegetable oil. These bring the oil-and-water principle within reach of younger students. For children, look for the AP seal of the Art and Creative Materials Institute, which marks paints and inks certified nontoxic. The hazard in an ink usually comes from its pigment rather than whether it is water- or oil-based. <cite>Fick and Grabowski, ch. 1</cite>

## Plates, presses, and inks

A monotype plate can be plexiglass, glass with taped edges, a gel plate, a baking sheet, or a laminated card. A lithograph can be drawn on stone, grained aluminum, a polyester plate, plywood (mokulito), sanded lino, or aluminum foil.

Monotype needs only hand pressure, a spoon, or a clean brayer. Lithography on stone and metal uses a scraper-bar press, while polyester plates and foil can be hand printed. Monotype works with water-based ink, tempera, acrylic, watercolor, or oil-based ink; lithography needs oil-based ink, since the whole process depends on oil and water repelling each other.
