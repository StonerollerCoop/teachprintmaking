## How it works

Intaglio is relief turned inside out. The printmaker makes marks below the surface of a plate, by scratching, cutting, or letting acid bite into metal. Ink is pushed down into those marks, and the flat surface is wiped clean. Damp paper is laid over the plate, and the two pass through a press under heavy pressure. The paper is forced down into the lines and lifts the ink out.

The wiping is where intaglio becomes its own craft. Wipe hard and only the lines print; leave a film of ink on the surface and the whole plate takes on a soft tone. Two impressions from the same plate can look very different, and students learn to read the plate as a set of choices rather than a fixed image.

Intaglio prints carry a sign of how they were made: the plate mark, a slight embossed edge where the plate pressed into the paper.

## A short history

Goldsmiths and armorers were cutting and etching lines into metal long before anyone printed from it. In the fifteenth century, engravers began inking copper plates and pressing paper into the grooves. Etching followed around 1500, when armor decorators' acid methods moved onto printing plates. The rolling press, with two rollers and a sliding bed, supplied the pressure intaglio needs.

Rembrandt combined etching and drypoint and kept reworking his plates, printing them in several states. Ludwig von Siegen made the first mezzotint in 1642, a method built for tone rather than line. Jean-Baptiste Le Prince developed aquatint in the 1760s, and Goya used it for his dark print series. In the late twentieth century, printmakers including Keith Howard and Friedhard Kiekeben developed safer methods using photopolymer film and metal salt etchants, which opened intaglio to places that could never house an acid room. <cite>Adams</cite>

## Ages, safety, and setting

Intaglio has a wide range. Drypoint on a juice box, a cereal box lined with plastic, or a sheet of acetate needs no chemicals at all and suits upper elementary students working with a press the teacher operates. Engraving with a burin and every form of etching belong to high school, college, and adult classes.

Traditional etching used nitric acid, solvent-based grounds, rosin dust, and chemicals now known to be dangerous. Older manuals recommend things that should never come back into a classroom, including asbestos boards, chloroform grounds, and dichromate etches. <cite>Ross and Romano 1974</cite> The safer methods of the last thirty years, such as ferric chloride on copper, copper sulfate and salt etches on zinc and aluminum, acrylic grounds, and photopolymer plates, are the right starting point for any school. Even these call for gloves, eye protection, ventilation, and a plan for disposing of spent etchant.

Scribes and burins are sharp and need the same care as carving tools.

## Plates, presses, and inks

A plate can be copper, zinc, steel, aluminum, plexiglass, acetate, a flattened juice box, the plastic-lined inside of a food carton, the lid of a foil cake tin, or a sealed cardboard collagraph. A press is the hardest part of intaglio to improvise, since the paper has to be forced into the lines. A pasta machine with felt and a plexiglass sandwich, an old clothes wringer for shallow lines, or a small 3D-printed press can all stand in for an etching press at a small scale.

Water-washable intaglio inks clean up with soap and water and suit schools. Tarlatan, a stiff open-weave cloth, wipes the ink across the plate; old phone-book pages or newsprint polish the surface clean.
