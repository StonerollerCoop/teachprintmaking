## How it works

A stencil blocks ink from some parts of a surface and lets it through to others. Cut a shape from paper, lay it down, and dab paint across it: the paint reaches the surface only through the opening. Lay a leaf down and roll ink over it, and the leaf becomes a stencil too, leaving its own shape blank.

A loose stencil has one problem. Any shape floating inside another, like the middle of a letter O, falls out. Stencil makers solved this with bridges, thin strips that hold the inside piece in place. Screen printing solved it differently, by attaching the stencil to a fine mesh stretched on a frame. The mesh holds every piece in position, and a squeegee pulls ink through the open areas.

A stencil print comes out the same way round as the stencil, so lettering never has to be cut backward.

## A short history

Stencils are the oldest prints we know. A hand stencil on a cave wall in Sulawesi, made by blowing or spraying pigment around a hand, is at least 67,800 years old, and hand stencils appear in caves on nearly every continent. Japanese craftspeople cut katagami stencils from layered mulberry paper to print resist patterns on kimono fabric, and Fijian makers cut stencils from banana and pandanus leaves to pattern barkcloth.

Thomas Edison's stencil patents led to the mimeograph, which schools and activists used for nearly a century. Samuel Simon patented silk screen printing in 1907, and artists in the New Deal Federal Art Project turned it into an artist's medium in the 1930s. Andy Warhol and Corita Kent brought screenprinting into Pop Art in the 1960s, and the Risograph, a stencil duplicator released in 1986, became a favorite of artists and zine makers.

## Ages, safety, and setting

A stencil print can be made by a three-year-old with a sponge and a paper shape, and a professional can spend a week preparing a photographic screen. Hand stencils, sponge stencils, and leaf stencils suit preschool and up. Cutting stencils with scissors suits the early grades; cutting with a craft knife suits middle school and up, with a cutting mat and a blade-safety talk. Simple screenprinting with paper stencils works from upper elementary, with an adult helping pull the squeegee.

Water-based screen inks clean up with water. Photo emulsion, its sensitizers, and screen-cleaning chemicals belong to older students with gloves and good ventilation. Spray paint should only be used outdoors or in a spray booth, with a mask rated for the job.

## Plates, presses, and inks

A stencil can be cut from copy paper, freezer paper, cardstock, oiled card, acetate, contact paper, leaves, tape, or lace. Found objects such as doilies, netting, and keys make instant stencils. A screen can be built from a picture frame or a set of canvas stretchers with mesh stapled across it.

Stencil printing needs no press. A sponge, a stencil brush, a dauber, a brayer, a toothbrush for spatter, or a squeegee moves the ink. Tempera, acrylic paint mixed with a screen-printing medium, water-based screen ink, and fabric ink all work, depending on what is being printed.
