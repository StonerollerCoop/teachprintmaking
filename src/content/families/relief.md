## How it works

A relief print comes from whatever stands highest on the block. The printmaker cuts, carves, or presses away the parts that should stay blank, rolls ink across the top, lays paper over it, and applies pressure. The ink transfers only where the surface touches the paper.

That makes relief the most intuitive family for students. They can see and feel the printing surface, and a thumb run across the block tells them what will print. It is also the family where the reversal of the image first surprises them: a block cut to read correctly prints backward, which is the first lesson of every letter carved by hand.

Relief needs very little pressure. A hand, a spoon, or a rolling pin is enough for most classroom blocks, which is why it is the usual starting point for printmaking at every age.

## A short history

Relief is the oldest family of printmaking. Carved cylinder seals rolled into clay in Mesopotamia carry the same idea as a rubber stamp, and woodblocks printed patterns on silk in China by about the third century. The earliest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra of 868, was printed from carved wooden blocks. In Europe, woodcuts appeared in the early fifteenth century as cheap devotional images, and Albrecht Dürer showed in the 1490s that a woodcut could carry the ambition of a painting.

Each century found new materials. Thomas Bewick cut fine detail into the end grain of boxwood in the 1790s. Japanese printmakers built color woodblock printing into a collaborative art, and Hokusai's Great Wave is its best-known image. Asante artisans in Ghana stamped adinkra cloth with carved calabash. Linoleum arrived as a floor covering in the 1860s and became the classroom's carving material in the twentieth century. Movable type, the root of letterpress, is relief printing too.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Relief reaches every age. Preschoolers can print with sponges, vegetables, and found objects. Children in the early grades can draw into foam with a pencil. Soft-cut blocks bring carving to upper elementary students, and linoleum and wood suit middle school and up.

The main hazard is the cutting tool. Teach students to keep both hands behind the blade and to use a bench hook, which holds the block against the table edge so the free hand never sits in front of the gouge. Water-based relief ink cleans up with soap and water and suits any classroom. Oil-based ink gives richer prints but needs vegetable oil or a solvent for cleanup; vegetable oil followed by soapy water is the safer choice. For children, look for the AP seal of the Art and Creative Materials Institute, which marks paints and inks certified nontoxic. The hazard in an ink usually comes from its pigment rather than whether it is water- or oil-based.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Almost anything with a flat, raised surface can be a relief plate: a potato, a cork, a dowel end, a gum eraser, a foam tray, layered cardboard, glued string, a leaf, a fish. Glue lines dried on cardboard print as raised lines. Textures glued to board build a collagraph.

For a press, a wooden spoon, the back of a rolling pin, a clean brayer, a homemade baren of cardboard wrapped in cloth, or a foot will all work. Tempera thickened with a little glycerin will cling to a block when proper relief ink isn't available, and stamp pads work for small blocks and found objects.
