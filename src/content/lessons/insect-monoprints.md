Oak National Academy wrote this sequence for Year 3 in England, which is roughly US second grade. It works well through grade 5.

## Session 1: Looking and drawing

Show close-up photographs of insects, or real specimens if you have them. Ask students to look for the main shapes first (head, thorax, abdomen, wings, legs) and then for pattern and texture.

1. Students make a few quick, light sketches of one insect.
2. They choose the strongest sketch and simplify it. Show them a detailed drawing next to a simplified one, and talk about why tiny details get lost when printing while bold lines and simple shapes come through.
3. They redraw the simplified version with clear outlines, then add a few patterns or textures with lines and dots. Making some lines darker and heavier helps the drawing stand out.

## Session 2: Monoprinting

### Set up

Give each table a tray with a small amount of ink and a brayer. Have paper cut a little smaller than the inked area.

### Trace monoprint

1. Roll a thin, even layer of ink on the tray. Too much ink will smudge the whole sheet.
2. Lay a clean sheet of paper gently on the ink without pressing.
3. Place the insect drawing on top and trace over it firmly with a pencil.
4. Peel the paper up and look at the underside. The lines will be soft and slightly fuzzy, and anywhere a hand rested will show as tone.

### Drawing into the ink

1. Re-roll the ink.
2. Draw into it with a cotton swab, the end of a brush, a fingertip, or a scrap of sponge. Wherever ink is removed will print white.
3. Lay paper on top, press all over with a flat hand, and peel.

Let students test their tools on a corner of the tray first so they can see how each one marks the ink.

## Talking about it

Students often expect a monoprint to come out the same every time. Put two prints side by side and ask why they differ. The answer is that each print takes ink that was laid down and moved by hand only once.

## Cleanup

Water-based ink washes off trays and brayers with warm water.

## Artists to look at

Oak's unit points to the illustrator Frances Gynn, who observes nature closely and uses detailed line to show form and texture.
