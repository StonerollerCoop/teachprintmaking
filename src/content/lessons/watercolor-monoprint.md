## Preparing the plate

Use frosted Mylar, or acrylic sheet lightly sanded with fine sandpaper so the watercolor has a tooth to sit on. Bevel and smooth the edges of thicker sheets. Clean the plate with powdered cleanser so it's free of grease. If you have gum arabic, spread a little over the plate and buff it to a thin film; it helps the paint flow on and release onto the paper, though the method works without it. Slip a guide drawing under the plate if students want one.

## Soaking the paper

Watercolor monoprint needs wetter paper than an ink print, because the moisture is what lifts the color. Soak sized printmaking paper for at least an hour before printing, then blot it.

## Painting the plate

Students paint with liquid watercolor, watercolor crayons, or watercolor pencils, and lift or soften marks with a damp brush, a cotton swab, or a rag. Two reminders help:

- The first marks laid down end up on top in the print, so delicate accents painted over thick color may disappear.
- Piling many layers on one plate turns colors muddy. Splitting the image across two or three plates keeps colors clear.

## Printing

1. Set up the press with blankets and a registration guide.
2. Place the plate, lay the blotted paper on it, cover with clean newsprint, and run it through.
3. For a multi-plate print, leave one edge of the paper caught under the roller, swap plates, and print again. Three plates is about the limit before the paper is too dry to lift the color.
4. If the paper starts to stick to the plate, mist the back lightly with water.

## Working the ghost

Paint usually remains on the plate after the first print. Students can rework that ghost with more watercolor and print it again, so one plate can grow into a series.

## Artists to look at

Fick and Grabowski feature Lisa Mackie, who prints watercolor monoprints on Mylar and reworks the ghosts into sequences of prints and artist's books.
