## What changes from the original

Students use the insect plate they already made and print it many times on one large sheet instead of making single prints.

## Looking at patterns

Show some tiled or printed fabric patterns and ask students how the shapes are arranged. Students often think a repeat pattern means stamping the same image anywhere. Point out that real patterns follow rules: a straight grid, a half-drop, a rotation, or a mirror.

## Steps

1. Lightly rule a grid on a large sheet of paper or card, or fold it into even sections.
2. Choose a rule for the pattern: every print facing the same way, every other print turned upside down, or each row shifted half a space.
3. Ink the plate and print it in the first square, then re-ink and continue across the sheet.
4. Try a second ink color on alternate squares if there's time.

Careful, even spacing is what makes a pattern read as a pattern, so remind students to line the plate up with the grid before pressing down.

## Cleanup

Wipe the plate gently with a damp sponge rather than running it under water.

## Artists to look at

Oak's lesson points to Yinka Shonibare, whose work is known for its bold patterned fabrics.
