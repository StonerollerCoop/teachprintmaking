## Before class

Cut foam blocks to a size that fits your paper with a margin, or cut flat panels from clean polystyrene food trays. Tear or cut printing paper a little larger than the blocks. Fill a shallow tray with water for dampening paper, and set out newsprint for blotting. Cover tables with newspaper and set up inking stations, each with a tray, a brayer, and a small amount of water-based ink.

Gather a few landscape and seascape prints to look at together. Katsushika Hokusai's woodblock prints work well, and museum open access collections have many in the public domain.

## Looking together (about 10 minutes)

Look at one landscape print closely as a class. Ask students what they notice first, where the horizon sits, and what is close, far, and in between. Point out that the artist simplified everything into shapes and lines, and that every line in the print was cut or pressed by hand. Ask how the print might have been made, then explain the relief process: the raised surface takes the ink, and anything pressed or cut away stays the color of the paper.

Brainstorm places students know: a park, a beach, a street, a view from a window.

## Demonstration (about 5 minutes)

Show the whole process once from start to finish so students see where they're headed. Make a point of showing that the print comes out reversed.

## Drawing and pressing the block (about 20 minutes)

1. Students sketch a landscape or seascape on scrap paper the size of their block, keeping shapes simple and bold.
2. They lay the sketch on the foam and trace firmly with a pencil so the lines dent the surface.
3. They lift the sketch away and go over every line again directly on the foam, pressing deep enough to make a clear groove without tearing through.

Lines that are too shallow will fill with ink and disappear, so encourage students to check their grooves by tilting the block toward the light.

## Dampening the paper

Dip each sheet in the water tray briefly, let it drip, then press it between sheets of newsprint so it is damp but not wet. Damp paper picks up more ink and gives a fuller print. If you'd rather skip this step, thin paper printed dry also works.

## Inking and printing (about 30 minutes)

1. Roll a small amount of ink on the tray until it is thin and even.
2. Roll the ink onto the block in more than one direction.
3. Lay the damp paper on the block and rub the back firmly with a baren or wooden spatula, covering every area.
4. Peel the paper away slowly from one corner and set the print to dry.
5. Re-ink and print again. Second and third prints are often better than the first.

## Cleanup (about 10 minutes)

Wash blocks gently with water if they'll be reused, and wash brayers and trays. Set wet prints on a drying rack or along a clear counter.

## Assessment and reflection

Ask students to explain the steps of relief printing in their own words and why the image is reversed. A simple rubric can give equal weight to craftsmanship, time on task, following directions, use of materials, and cleanup.

## Extensions

Print the same block on different colored papers, or print a second color with a separate block cut for the sky or water.
