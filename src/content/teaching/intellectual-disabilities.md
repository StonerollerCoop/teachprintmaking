Printmaking has a long life in studios for artists with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Creative Growth in Oakland lists printmaking among its studio media. <cite>Creative Growth</cite> At NIAD Art Center in Richmond, California, Andrés Cisneros-Galindo has worked with artists on prints since 1985. <cite>Richmond Art Center</cite> At Edinburgh Printmakers, adults from Garvald's day services took masterclasses in drypoint, copper etching, screenprinting, and collagraph. <cite>Edinburgh Printmakers</cite> There is no ceiling on what these artists can learn.

## Break it into steps

Printmaking is already a sequence, which helps. Write out every step by watching yourself do it, and check that each step is a single action in words the learner understands. <cite>AFIRM, Task Analysis</cite> Steps can be taught from first to last, or from the end backward, with the learner doing the last step on their own first. In a print class that might mean the learner pulls the paper off the plate and sees the print, while a helper inks, until the learner is ready to take over the inking too.

## Show the steps

Put the steps where the learner can see them: a strip of photos, drawings, or words, or the actual objects in order. Hand the schedule to the learner and stand behind them, so they look at the card and not at you. Tape on the table can show where the inking area ends and the printing area begins. <cite>AFIRM, Visual Supports</cite> One teacher adapting a collagraph unit for an inclusive second-grade class gave the students a printed step booklet, so each student and aide could move at their own pace toward the same goals as everyone else. <cite>Marginalia</cite>

Demonstrate each stage on the day it happens, and show what correctly rolled ink looks like before anyone starts. <cite>Crockett</cite>

## Choose repeatable processes

Foam plates, gel plates, stamps, and found objects can be inked and printed again and again, so a learner can repeat a step until it feels sure. A blotted monoprint needs no planning at all and gives a result in seconds. <cite>Martin, p. 24</cite> Repetition builds skill and confidence, and every pull is still a print.

## Support lightly

Begin with the smallest help that might work, such as a single pre-drawn line, and add more only if needed. <cite>Naughton 2021</cite> Hand-under-hand guidance is gentler than hand-over-hand, and full physical help should be used only with the learner's agreement. <cite>Coleman</cite>

## The image is theirs

If an aide makes the print, the learner hasn't. <cite>Coleman</cite> The art center model founded by Florence Ludins-Katz and Elias Katz gives artists materials and time and lets them work from their own ideas at their own pace. <cite>Finley</cite> Artists with intellectual disabilities should have choice and control over the process. <cite>Miller, Fernholz, and Siegel</cite>

There is a real difference between teaching a process step by step and facilitating an artist's own work. Step cards come from skill-teaching practice, and studios like these lean toward stepping back. Both have a place. A step card can teach how a press works while every choice about the image stays with the artist.

## Presume competence

Speak to the learner, not about them. Describe what a person can do and what they need, and avoid predictions about what they will never learn. <cite>Brookes</cite> For adults, use adult materials and adult subjects; a professional press and good paper say that the work matters.
