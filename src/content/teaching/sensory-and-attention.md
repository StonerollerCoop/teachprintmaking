A print room is full of strong sensations. Oil ink and solvents smell, ink is sticky, presses clank, and a busy room with drying racks and wet prints everywhere is a lot to take in. None of that has to keep anyone out. A few changes make the room calmer for the students who need it, and most other students will appreciate them too.

The Autistic Self Advocacy Network and many autistic people prefer "autistic person," and some people prefer other wording. <cite>ASAN</cite> Follow each learner's own words.

## Sensitivity runs both ways

Some people are overwhelmed by a sense, such as a sudden noise, a strong smell, or a texture. Others seek more of it and may want to press hard, roll loudly, or get their hands into the ink. Many people are both, depending on the sense and the day. A student who avoids an activity may not dislike it at all. <cite>National Autistic Society</cite>

## Smell

Water-based inks need no solvents, which removes the strongest smell in a print room. Oil-based ink comes off hands with baby oil or vegetable oil instead of solvent. <cite>Princeton EHS</cite> A student can keep a tissue with a favorite scent nearby to mask smells they can't stand. <cite>NHS Borders</cite>

## Touch

Offer tools for anything messy: brayers, barens, spoons, and craft sticks keep ink off the hands. Keep latex-free gloves on every table for anyone who wants them, and put wipes or a basin close by. <cite>Art of Education; NHS Borders</cite> Some students would rather be the clean-hands person who places and lifts the paper. Always ask before touching a student. <cite>NHS Borders</cite>

## Sound

Warn students before a loud sound, such as a press bed rolling back or a screen frame dropping. Soft surfaces cut background noise, and ear defenders or headphones help some students stay in the room. <cite>NHS Borders; National Autistic Society</cite>

## Space and light

Clear what you don't need off the counters and walls. Offer a desk divider or a quiet corner that any student can choose, and keep it available for someone who needs to recover from overload, with few words and time to settle. <cite>Art of Education; National Autistic Society</cite> Edinburgh Printmakers moved its sessions with autistic artists into a quieter studio and found structured, routine sessions suited them. <cite>Edinburgh Printmakers</cite>

## Make it predictable

Use the same order every session. Show the plan with a visual schedule or a first-then card, give one direction at a time, and use a visual timer so a student can see how long is left. <cite>NHS Borders; AFIRM</cite>

## Short cycles and quick results

Short timed work periods with brief breaks help many students with ADHD, along with frequent feedback and a regular job. <cite>CHADD</cite> Monoprinting suits this well: roll ink on a plate, draw into it, and pull a print in a few minutes. A blotted monoprint needs no planning at all. <cite>Martin, p. 24</cite> Gel plates, stamps, and found objects also give a print almost at once. A mono collagraph works the same way: roll ink onto a sealed board, lay leaves or lace on it, and print. <cite>Major-George, p. 47</cite>

Some processes forgive a break better than others. Silvie Turner noted that relief printing doesn't suffer from a pause of five minutes or so, while in screen printing a break of even a couple of minutes causes problems, because the ink starts drying in the mesh. <cite>Turner 1983, p. 64</cite> For a student who needs to step away, relief and monotype are the safer choices, and a screenprint is better left until the student is ready for a short, steady run.

Jobs give structure to a busy room. A student can be the inker, the printer, the paper handler, or the person who hangs prints to dry, and the roles can rotate. Choices help too, such as where to sit, which color to use, or which job to take. <cite>CHADD</cite>

## Neurodiversity-affirming practice

Accept that there is more than one right way to think, move, and communicate. Don't discourage stimming that harms no one, don't require eye contact, use the least prompting needed, and respect a refusal when it is safe. Avoid rewards or punishments used to force compliance. <cite>Autism Queensland</cite>

## Older adults living with dementia

Printmaking is used in dementia-friendly programs because it gives a finished image without needing much drawing skill. The Wallace Collection in London pairs looking at portraits with making a monoprint, and Northern Print in Newcastle runs relaxed printmaking sessions for people with dementia and their carers. <cite>Wallace Collection; Northern Print</cite> Keep projects at an adult level, avoid sharp tools, allow plenty of time, and talk with people about what they are making. <cite>Alzheimer's Association</cite>
