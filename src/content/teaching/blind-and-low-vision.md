Printmaking may be the most tactile of the visual arts. A relief block or a collagraph plate is a landscape of high and low places, and an intaglio print lifts its lines up off the paper like a cast. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. xvii, 134–135</cite> A blind student can read a plate with their fingers before it is printed and read the print afterward.

Blind printmakers are working now. Joe Monteleone, a Deafblind artist in Australia, makes linocuts and has said he prefers them because they are tactile. <cite>Arts Access Victoria</cite> At Edinburgh Printmakers, the visually impaired artist Kwok Kin Chan worked through collagraph, etching, and other processes with one-to-one support and tactile methods. <cite>Edinburgh Printmakers</cite>

Many blind people prefer to be called blind, and the National Federation of the Blind has said it is respectable to be blind. <cite>NFB</cite> Others prefer different words. Ask.

## Build up the plate

Cutting away a block to leave a raised image is hard to plan by touch. Building the image up is easier: glue card on card, lay down string or yarn, or draw raised lines with thick glue. <cite>Macular Society</cite> The student feels their design directly as they make it, and the plate prints the same shapes they touched. Collage blocks of string, sandpaper, dried beans, rice, buttons, and tape all read clearly by hand and print well. <cite>Martin, pp. 54–59; Heller 1958, p. 97</cite>

Wikki Stix, the wax-covered yarn sticks, make a removable raised drawing on a wipeable board. Roll tempera over them with a foam brayer, lay paper on top, press, and lift. The same design can be inked again in a new color or used for a crayon rubbing. <cite>School Specialty; Paths to Literacy</cite>

## Design for the fingers

Tactile graphics, the raised diagrams used in braille books, follow rules that work for a printing plate too. <cite>BANA; APH</cite>

- Keep it simple and keep the main idea.
- Use only a few different textures on one plate, about five at most, and make them clearly different from each other.
- Leave a gap, about an eighth of an inch, between textures and where lines cross.
- Make shapes big enough to feel, at least about a quarter inch across.
- Leave open space so fingers can move across the plate.
- Drop decorative borders unless the edge helps the reader find their way.

## Teach touch first

Start with real objects, then move toward flat images. Press an object into clay or playdough and feel the hollow it leaves, then trace the object, then make it into a plate. Explore a plate with both hands, get an overview before the details, and begin from the top left. <cite>Schwartz and Brooks</cite> That path from object to impression to print is the logic of printmaking itself.

A graphite rubbing of a relief block gives a proof with no ink and no press, and a blind embossing, made by printing a block with no ink at all on damp paper, gives a print that is entirely raised. <cite>Martin, p. 31; Heller 1958, pp. 91–92</cite> For a deep embossing, Kim Major-George builds a collagraph from firm materials with smooth tops, such as layered card, oats, filler, glue drops, and string, leaves out anything sharp, and lets the print dry in the air so the relief stays raised. <cite>Major-George, p. 45</cite> She also keeps the raised parts of a plate at an even height. <cite>Major-George, p. 16</cite> A plate made that way is easier to explore by hand.

Intaglio offers its own tactile signs. The plate mark, the dent the plate leaves in damp paper, is what Francis Tinsley calls the trademark of an etching. On an engraving, he notes, the burr can be felt along the line before printing. <cite>Tinsley 1983, pp. 36, 59</cite> A student can find the plate mark by running a finger around the margin of the print.

Some cues in the print room are heard rather than seen. Rolled ink is ready when it makes a slightly tacky sound, and the same sound comes back when the block is fully inked. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 90</cite> That is a working signal a blind student can use without help.

The artist Phyllis McGibbon etched a copper tube with what she describes as a Braille-like image, which prints a trace when it is rolled along a surface. She writes about prints made from the need to touch. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, pp. 212–213</cite>

## Set up the room

For students with low vision, use a dark or colored mat under white paper, outline the work area with painter's tape, keep supplies back from the table edge, and hold small things in egg cartons or cup holders. Bright, saturated colors are easier to see than pastels, and task lighting with little glare helps. A student's own magnifier, a video magnifier, or a tablet's zoom can stay at the table. <cite>Lewis, Perkins</cite>

For students with little or no sight, tape the paper down and add a raised edge so the boundary can be felt, and put each material in a differently shaped container. <cite>Naughton 2025</cite> Keep the room in the same order every session, and label trays with tactile marks or braille. <cite>Macular Society</cite>

## Describe everything

Tell a student what they are about to touch before they touch it. <cite>Macular Society</cite> When you describe a demonstration or a print, start with the most important thing, move through it in a steady direction, describe what is there before you interpret it, and use plain color names. <cite>Cooper Hewitt</cite>

## Cutting and registration

If a student wants to carve, a soft block, a bench hook, and a cutter that pulls toward a stop all keep the other hand out of the way. <cite>School Specialty</cite> Blind people use touch-based safety habits in woodshops, and a teacher can learn them from the student.

We haven't yet found a published method for registering prints by touch. Corner stops on a jig that the plate and paper butt against, a notch cut in one corner of the plate, and raised tape lines as paper guides are our suggestions. The Japanese kento, a corner stop and a side stop cut into the block itself, is a sighted method that may also work well by touch, since the paper drops into place against two edges. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 29</cite> If you have a method that works, please send it in.
