Drawing asks a student to make every decision with the pencil. A print splits those decisions into steps you can see and hold. That makes printmaking one of the clearest ways to teach the basics of design, and it works at every age.

## Positive and negative space

In a relief print, everything you cut away prints as paper and everything you leave prints as ink. A student holding a gouge has to decide, area by area, which is figure and which is ground. Cutting a shape out of the block leaves it white on black; cutting around it leaves it black on white. <cite>Martin, p. 28</cite>

A cut paper stencil makes the same point in one move. Print through the hole, then use the shape that came out of it as a mask and print again. The two prints are opposites, and students see figure and ground trade places. <cite>Martin, p. 69</cite>

Try this before any carving: draw only the shapes of the spaces around an object, then draw the object itself, and compare. Susie Hodge uses it with sculpture, and it prepares students well for a block. <cite>Hodge, p. 134</cite>

## Value

Arthur Wesley Dow taught design through notan, the Japanese term for the arrangement of dark and light, using exercises drawn from woodblock prints. <cite>Dow</cite> A block print forces the same thinking. The first print is often only black and white, so students learn to build a light, a middle, and a dark with lines and textures. A reduction print adds values one color at a time, from lightest to darkest.

## Reversal

A print is a mirror of its plate. Students who carve a word discover this the hard way, and the discovery is worth more than a warning. Hold a small mirror up to the drawing before it goes onto the block, or trace it, flip the tracing, and transfer it backward. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 12, 31</cite> Monoprints traced from the back, and offset lithography, are useful contrasts because the image reverses twice and comes out reading the right way.

## Color

Printing ink is often transparent, so overprinting mixes colors right on the paper. Yellow printed over blue makes a different green from blue printed over yellow, which turns the order of printing into a design decision. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 92</cite> Each added color doubles the number of possible combinations, so two colors and the paper give four, and three give eight. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 28</cite> Printing cyan, magenta, and yellow over each other connects the classroom to the way books and magazines are printed. <cite>Martin, pp. 70–73</cite>

## Pattern and repetition

A block can be printed again and again, which makes pattern feel natural. Repeat a small block in a straight grid, then in a half-drop where each row shifts by half a block, then rotate it. <cite>Kafka</cite> Ian Mowforth cut nine square blocks that can be printed in any order, which turns a class into a pattern machine: each student cuts one square, and the class arranges and prints them together. <cite>Martin, p. 134</cite>

## Composition

Because a print can be pulled many times, a student can compare one image printed on different papers, in different colors, or cropped by a mask. Judy Martin's book prints one subject in many processes so their textures can be compared side by side, and the same exercise works in a class. <cite>Martin, pp. 12–13</cite>
