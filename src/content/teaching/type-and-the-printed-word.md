Every letter on a screen descends from a letter that once had to be cut, cast, or carved and then printed. When students make letters the same way, the vocabulary of typography stops being abstract.

## Carving letters

Libby Clarke teaches leading and typesetting by having students carve their own letters and learn the baseline and typography through printing them.

<span class="flag">Libby to describe how the lesson runs in her own words.</span>

Letters for a cut block work best when they are designed for the tool, and Francis Kafka's 1955 manual includes alphabets drawn for the cutting line. <cite>Kafka</cite> Thick strokes and open counters survive the gouge, while fine serifs tend to break off. Screenprinted letters follow a similar rule: bold, plain letterforms hold up in a hand-cut stencil, and small or thin letters fill in. <cite>Ross, Romano, and Ross 1990</cite>

## Setting and spacing

Once students have carved letters, real type makes sense. A composing stick, a case of type, and a few lines of spacing material show that every gap on a page is a physical piece of metal or wood. A linoleum block can be printed alongside set type if it is mounted to type-high, 0.918 inch, the standard height of type in the United States. <cite>Kafka</cite>

## Ways around reversal

Some processes let students write the right way round. Text written on homemade transfer paper and transferred to a lithographic plate prints reading correctly. <cite>Tamarind 2008</cite> Words typed onto a mimeograph stencil can be taped into a silk screen and printed. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 230–231</cite> Letter stencils cut from contact paper or tape work at any age. <cite>Ross, Romano, and Ross 1990</cite>

## Why lettering choices matter

Susie Hodge starts a lettering lesson by asking groups to rank lettering styles and then ask whether their choice would change for a shop sign, a bedroom door, or a public building. <cite>Hodge, p. 59</cite> The same question carries straight into a print project: who will read this, and where?

## The printed word in history

The history of print is the history of who gets to read and who gets to speak. Gutenberg's press is the usual starting point, and students should also meet the earlier printed books of East Asia. In Mexico, José Guadalupe Posada's broadsides paired pictures with ballads and news for readers on the street, and the Taller de Gráfica Popular carried that tradition into linoleum posters in the twentieth century. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 62–63</cite> The WPA poster workshops of the 1930s screenprinted public messages by the thousand, and the Library of Congress holds more than nine hundred of them. A school newspaper printed on a mimeograph belongs to the same story.
