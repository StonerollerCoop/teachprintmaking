## Demonstrate up close

Printmaking is learned by watching hands. A student needs to see how much ink goes on the slab, how the brayer sounds when the layer is right, and how the paper is lifted from the block. Gather the class around one table so nobody is stuck at the back, set out everything before they arrive, and name each tool as you pick it up. Then ask a few students to tell you the next step before you do it, which shows you who has it and who needs another look. <cite>Hodge, pp. 71, 76, 119</cite>

Keep a print from every stage on hand: the drawing, the plate, the first proof, and a finished print. Students understand reversal and inking much faster when they can hold the plate next to its print.

## Set up stations

A print room runs on traffic. Give each color one inking station with a tray and two brayers, so only a few people ink at a time, and post the steps where everyone can see them. Show what correctly rolled ink looks like: a fine, even texture like orange peel, with no shine and no ridges. <cite>Crockett</cite>

Many teachers split the room into inky hands and clean hands. Inking happens at one table, printing at another, and paper is handled only by clean hands. A drying rack or a line with clothespins should be set up before the first print is pulled.

Silvie Turner's rule for the bench is strict and useful: if you spill ink or find it on your arms, stop, clean up, and start again. <cite>Turner 1983, p. 64</cite> Paper fingers, two small strips of folded paper or card, let an inky-handed printer lift a clean sheet without marking it. <cite>Turner 1983, p. 84</cite>

Kim Major-George ran collagraph workshops for more than two hundred children over four days. Water-soluble ink, newspaper in place of tissue for wiping, and a cheaper paper that needs only a short soak made that pace possible. <cite>Major-George, pp. 22, 24, 28–29</cite>

## Leave time for clean-up

Clean-up takes longer in printmaking than in drawing, and a class that runs out of time leaves ink drying on brayers. Plan it into the session as its own step, with a job for each table and a chart on the wall. <cite>Hodge, p. 81</cite> Water-based inks wash up at the sink. Oil-based inks come off with vegetable oil and then soap, with no solvents.

## Proofs are for learning

A proof is a test print, and a printmaker takes many of them. Ask students to keep their proofs in order, write on them what they changed, and draw on a proof with chalk or pencil to plan the next cuts. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 79, 157</cite> The stack of proofs shows the thinking, and it gives you something to assess that isn't only the final print.

## Talking about the prints

Near the end of a session, put the prints up together and walk the room. Ask each student to choose one print that isn't theirs and say what works in it and what they'd try next. <cite>Hodge, p. 22</cite> Comments without grades help students focus on how to improve, and it helps to agree on what you are looking for at the start of the project, in words the students themselves use. <cite>Hodge, pp. 21–26</cite>

Give everyone time to think before anyone answers. A "no hands up" rule, or a short wait before you call on someone, brings in the students who need a moment. <cite>Hodge, pp. 22, 67</cite>

## A first day that builds a class

Jules Heller opened his lithography course by having every student make a mark on one freshly grained stone, with the last student pulling the marks together into a single image that was printed within the hour. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 244–245</cite> The same idea works with a large foam plate or a gel plate: everyone adds something, and the class sees its first print together.
