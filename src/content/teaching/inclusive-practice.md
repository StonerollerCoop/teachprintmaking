Printmaking is already one of the most open ways into art. A print can be made by pressing a leaf, rolling a brayer, or standing on a block. It can be read by touch, and it can be made by a whole team working together. The pages in this section are about using that openness on purpose.

## Ask first

The person in front of you knows more about what works for them than any guide does. <cite>DO-IT</cite> Ask what helps, ask again when something changes, and ask how they describe themselves. Some blind people prefer "blind person" and some autistic people prefer "autistic person," while others prefer person-first wording, and individual preference comes first. <cite>APA Style</cite> The disability rights movement put this as "nothing about us without us." <cite>Charlton</cite>

## Look for the barrier

The social model of disability, developed by disabled people, holds that people are disabled by barriers around them more than by their impairments. <cite>Scope</cite> Not everyone uses it, but it is a practical question for a print teacher: what in this room, this tool, or this process is in the way? A carving tool that needs a strong grip is a barrier, and so is a demonstration that only works if you can see it. Both can be changed.

## Design for many ways in

Universal Design for Learning asks teachers to offer more than one way to take in information, more than one way to stay engaged, and more than one way to show what you know. <cite>CAST</cite> In a print room, a single demonstration can be spoken aloud, written on a step card, and handed around as a plate to touch. A finished print can be looked at, felt, and talked about.

## Lightest support first

Kim Major-George tells how one of her students, Dawn, couldn't get on with the tissue squares used to wipe a collagraph because of her long nails. Dawn made her own padded dabber from half a sponge scrubber wrapped in cloth, and Major-George named it after her and teaches it in her book. <cite>Major-George, p. 25</cite> The learner often knows what tool would work, and a change made for one person can end up helping everyone.

Start with the smallest change that might work, such as a pre-drawn line, a taped edge, or a softer block, and add more only if it's needed. Ability can vary from day to day. <cite>Naughton</cite> Hand-under-hand guidance, where the learner's hand rests on yours, is less intrusive than hand-over-hand, and full physical help should happen only with the learner's agreement. <cite>Coleman</cite>

## The art belongs to the artist

Presume competence. Speak to the learner directly, not through an aide, and use language and subjects that suit their age. <cite>Brookes</cite> If a helper ends up making the print, the student hasn't made it. Every adaptation should give the learner more control over the work, never less. <cite>Coleman</cite>

## Hands and grip

Some changes help almost everyone. Foam plates and soft carving blocks need no strength, and many need no cutting at all. A bench hook holds the block so both hands stay behind the blade. Built-up handles, foam grips, Velcro straps, and non-slip mats help learners with limited grip or a tremor. <cite>Coleman</cite> A press that turns with a wheel needs less force than burnishing with a spoon, and standing on a block or rolling over it with a rolling pin are other options.
