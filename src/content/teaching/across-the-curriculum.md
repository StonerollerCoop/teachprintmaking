A print records something real: the scales of a fish, the veins of a leaf, the words of a ballad. That makes it a natural partner for other subjects. These are starting points that teachers of other subjects can share with the art room.

## Science

**Nature printing.** Gyotaku, the Japanese tradition of printing fish, began as a way for anglers to record their catch, and it doubles as a lesson in anatomy. Leaves printed on a gel plate or rolled with ink and pressed show vein patterns that are hard to see by eye. Ask students to label what they find.

**The chemistry of ink.** Purple cabbage water turns pink with vinegar and green-blue with baking soda, so a homemade ink becomes a pH test. Tea, walnut hulls, and acorn caps contain tannin, and a little iron from a rusty nail darkens them, which is the reaction behind the iron gall ink used for centuries of manuscripts. <cite>Logan</cite> The [ink page](/parts/ink/) has the safety notes.

**Observation and place.** Jason Logan asks workshop participants to study a single square metre of ground and find every color in it. <cite>Logan</cite> On school grounds, that becomes an ecology lesson that ends in a set of inks and prints.

## Math

Registration is measurement. Students who line up two colors have to measure, mark corners, and check their work. Symmetry and tessellation come alive when a block is printed, flipped, and rotated. Color overprinting is a doubling sequence, since each new color doubles the number of possible combinations. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 28</cite> In etching, aquatint bite times are often set as 1, 4, and 16 minutes, a geometric progression that older students can graph against the tones it produces. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 148</cite>

## History and social studies

Prints carried news, protest, and devotion long before photography. Posada's broadsides and the posters of the Taller de Gráfica Popular connect well with Spanish and Latin American history. Japanese ukiyo-e prints were made by a team of designer, block cutter, and printer, which opens a conversation about work and trade. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 62–66</cite> The WPA posters at the Library of Congress show how the United States government used the silk screen during the Depression.

## Reading and writing

A printed book is a sequence, and so is a set of prints. Wordless novels told in woodcuts, such as those by Frans Masereel and Lynd Ward, invite students to read pictures in order and then make their own short sequence. <cite>Walker</cite> Students who print their own small books learn about pages, folding, and binding along the way.

## Technology

Process color printing, the cyan, magenta, yellow, and black dots in every magazine, is the same overprinting students do by hand with transparent inks. <cite>Martin, pp. 70–73</cite> A magnifying glass on a newspaper photograph makes the link.
