## How it works

Every lithograph rests on the same principle: grease attracts oily ink, a treated bare surface holds water, and water repels the ink. Stone and grained aluminum are the classic surfaces, but several newer methods apply the principle to cheaper and safer materials.

**Polyester plates.** A thin plastic plate with a specially treated surface accepts drawing in ballpoint pen, permanent marker, litho crayon, or laser-printer toner. The plate is wiped with water, inked with a brayer, and printed <span class="flag">by hand with a spoon or baren, or</span> on an etching press. Toothpaste works as a gentle eraser on these plates. Because laser prints can be transferred straight onto the plate, polyester plates are a natural fit for design classes and for type. <cite>Devon, Lagattuta, and Hamon, ch. 9; Hughes and Vernon-Morris, ch. 12</cite>

**Kitchen lithography.** Aluminum foil smoothed over a rigid board is drawn on with an oily crayon, soap, or a greasy pencil. Cola, which contains phosphoric acid, acts as the etch. The foil is rinsed, dampened, and rolled with oil-based ink, which clings only to the drawing. <span class="flag">The French artist Émilie Aizier popularized the method.</span>

**Mokulito.** Wood lithography <span class="flag">developed in Japan in the 1970s</span>. A smooth sheet of plywood is drawn on with greasy materials and treated with gum arabic, and the wood holds water just as stone does. The grain of the wood shows in the print. <cite>Dalton, ch. 5</cite>

**Lithino.** Lino sanded smooth can be drawn on and printed lithographically, even partway through a reduction linocut, so carved relief and drawn tone share one block. <span class="flag">The printmaker Mary Dalton developed the gentle sanding approach</span> described in her book Expressive Printmaking. <cite>Dalton, ch. 6</cite>

**Transfer paper.** Paper brushed with a thin coating of gum arabic and glycerin can be drawn on and then transferred face down onto a plate. The drawing reverses twice, once onto the plate and once onto the paper, so writing done the right way round prints the right way round. <cite>Devon, Lagattuta, and Hamon, ch. 9</cite> Heller gives an older homemade transfer paper coated with dental plaster and flour. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 246</cite>

**Paper lithography.** A black-and-white photocopy can serve as the plate. The toner image is greasy enough to take oil-based ink, and the bare paper, wiped with a thin mix of gum arabic and water, holds water and rejects it. The copy is laid face up, sponged, inked with a brayer, then laid face down on the printing paper and run through a press. Each copy usually gives only a couple of prints. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, pp. 183–184</cite> Because a copy is cheap, students can make several of the same image, and it is one of the most immediate ways to show a class how lithography works.

## History

Lithography was discovered on stone in 1796, and metal plates were in use by the nineteenth century. Polyester plates came from the office printing industry. Mokulito developed in Japan in the 1970s, and kitchen lithography and lithino are twenty-first-century methods spread by printmakers through workshops, books, and online videos.

Experiments away from the stone have a longer history than they seem to. In 1958 Heller noted that glass, iron, and rubber will all take a lithographic image, and he recorded Albert W. Barker's method of lithography with no etch and T. E. Griffits's method of transfer printing with magnesium chloride. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 36 and 244</cite>

## Ages, safety, and setting

Kitchen lithography suits middle school and up, with the teacher handling the cola etch and the ink. Polyester plates suit high school students and can be printed with a spoon. Mokulito and lithino suit college and adult classes.

All of these use oil-based ink. Clean up with vegetable oil, then soap and water, and avoid solvents. Mokulito and lithino involve sanding; wear a dust mask and sand away from others.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: polyester lithography plates, heavy-duty aluminum foil wrapped over a rigid board, birch plywood, and sanded linoleum.

Drawing materials: ballpoint pen, permanent marker, laser toner, litho crayon, oil pastel, soap, and lipstick.

Presses: a wooden spoon, a baren, or a clean brayer for foil and polyester plates; an etching press for a stronger print.

Inks: oil-based relief or lithographic ink. Water-based ink won't work, since the method depends on oil and water repelling.

<span class="flag">Polyester plates need a laser printer, since inkjet ink doesn't hold up. Keep halftones coarse, around 75 lines per inch or less. A water bath with a little gum arabic and citric acid keeps the plate clean while printing.</span> <cite>Fick and Grabowski, ch. 7; Hughes and Vernon-Morris, ch. 12</cite>

Grabowski and Fick list many more ways to draw on a polyester plate: china marker, litho crayon, India ink, carbon black acrylic, acrylic floor polish tinted with India ink, and washes of toner. Gum arabic or a water-soluble pencil drawn on first works as a mask that washes away and leaves white. The plate can be scraped or erased between prints to change the image. Because it is translucent, a second color plate can be laid face down onto the print by eye. On an etching press, take out the soft blanket and print under a stiff sheet. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, pp. 185–186</cite>

Paper lithographs last longer if the back of the copy is brushed with shellac. Keep the copy wet the whole time, loosen the ink so it doesn't pull the toner off, and expect a little tone in the empty areas; it can be planned for. A pinch of white in black ink makes the roll-up easier to see. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, pp. 183–184</cite>

Plate lithography on zinc and aluminum is the subject of Nicholas Hacking's section of Practical Printmaking. He compares the two metals and explains that ready-grained plates come in grades from coarse to very fine, and that one graining serves two or three drawings. He also makes transfer paper by spraying a thin layer of gum arabic onto smooth drawing paper. <cite>Hacking 1983, pp. 18, 22</cite> His etches use chemicals that don't belong in a school, so take the principle and leave the recipes.
