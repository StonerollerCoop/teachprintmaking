## How it works

The printmaker draws on a grained limestone or aluminum plate with greasy materials: lithographic crayons in grades from hard to soft, rubbing crayon applied with a cloth, and tusche, a greasy ink that can be brushed, penned, or diluted into washes. Tusche washes break up into lacy patterns called reticulation, and salt, alcohol, or soda added to a wash change how it dries.

The drawing is then processed. A coat of gum arabic, made slightly acidic, is applied and buffed thin. This etch fixes the grease into the surface and makes the bare areas hold water. The drawing material itself is washed out with solvent, leaving an invisible greasy image in the stone, and the printer rolls it up with ink until the image returns at full strength.

To print, the surface is sponged with water, which sits on the bare areas, and rolled with oily ink, which sticks only to the image. Paper is laid on and passed through a scraper-bar press, which drags a leather-covered bar across the back of the sheet under great pressure. <cite>Antreasian and Adams, chs. 1–2</cite>

Color lithographs use a separate stone or plate for each color, registered with marks or pins. <cite>Antreasian and Adams, ch. 7</cite>

Conté, charcoal, and ordinary graphite don't print, because they hold no grease. A light color is drawn as a solid dark image and printed in pale ink; the drawing itself is always black on the stone. <cite>Hughes and Vernon-Morris, ch. 10; Fick and Grabowski, ch. 7</cite>

## History

Alois Senefelder discovered lithography in Bavaria in 1796. It became the great commercial printing process of the nineteenth century and the medium of the color poster; Toulouse-Lautrec's Moulin Rouge poster of 1891 turned the street into a gallery. By the 1950s, artist lithography was nearly extinct in the United States, and in 1960 June Wayne founded the Tamarind Lithography Workshop in Los Angeles to train master printers. Tamarind, now at the University of New Mexico, and the workshops its printers founded revived lithography as an artist's medium. <cite>Devon, Lagattuta, and Hamon</cite>

## Ages, safety, and setting

Stone and metal-plate lithography belong in a college or adult studio. Stones are heavy and need two people to lift. Processing uses gum arabic, mild acids, solvents, rosin, and talc. Older books recommend materials that should never be used now, including gasoline, benzine, phenol, dichromate compounds, and lead-based pigments. Current practice relies on ventilation, gloves, goggles, citrus or soy-based cleaners in place of turpentine, and phosphoric or citric acid etches where possible. <cite>Devon, Lagattuta, and Hamon, ch. 2</cite>

For school students, the polyester plate and kitchen lithography methods described in [Lithography without a stone](/types/planographic/lithography-without-a-stone/) teach the same principle safely.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: Bavarian limestone, grained aluminum plates, and polyester plates. Old stones can be regrained with carborundum grit and reused many times.

Drawing materials: lithographic crayons and pencils, rubbing crayon, and stick or liquid tusche. Unconventional greasy materials such as lipstick, soap, oil pastel, and ballpoint pen also print.

Presses: a scraper-bar lithography press. Small aluminum and polyester plates can be printed on an etching press with a sheet of plastic over the paper.

Inks: oil-based lithographic ink. Magnesium carbonate stiffens it, and transparent base makes tints.

The color of a stone shows its hardness. Pale yellow stones are softer and suit bold work; gray and blue-gray stones are harder and hold fine detail. Small stones should be at least two inches thick, and large stones three or four, or they may crack under the press. <cite>Hughes and Vernon-Morris, ch. 10</cite>
