## How it works

A monotype starts with a smooth, nonabsorbent plate. The printmaker paints on it with ink, rolls it with ink and wipes or scratches out the lights, or combines both. Paper is laid on top and pressed, and the image transfers. Most of the ink leaves the plate in the first print, so each monotype is one of a kind. A second, paler print from the leftover ink is called a ghost, and many printmakers rework the ghost into a new image.

Working additively, the printmaker paints the image onto a clean plate. Working subtractively, the printmaker rolls the plate solid and removes ink with rags, cotton swabs, brushes, combs, or a fingertip, drawing light out of dark.

In a trace monotype, the plate is rolled with a thin layer of ink, paper is laid on top, and the printmaker draws on the back of the paper. Wherever the pencil presses, ink transfers, giving a soft, grainy line. Anything that touches the paper prints as well, including the side of a hand, which students learn to use on purpose.

Printers use monoprint for a print that has a repeated element, such as a carved block or a stencil, combined with painted or rolled ink that changes each time. Both words are often used loosely for the same thing.

A color monotype can be made from three clear acetate plates, one painted for each of three transparent colors, registered by a mark in one corner and printed one over another. In a watercolor monoprint, a plastic plate is sanded lightly so watercolor will hold on it, then printed onto paper soaked until soft; the water in the paper lifts the paint.

## History

Giovanni Benedetto Castiglione made the first monotypes in Genoa in the 1640s, painting and wiping ink on copper. Edgar Degas made hundreds of monotypes in the 1870s and 1880s, often working over them in pastel. Paul Gauguin developed trace monotype. In the twentieth century, monotype became one of the most popular forms of printmaking in schools because it needs no carving and no press.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Monotype suits every age. Preschoolers can finger-paint on plexiglass and press paper onto it. Trace monotype works at desks and suits upper elementary and up.

Water-based ink and tempera clean up with water. Oil-based ink stays workable longer and gives richer color; clean it up with vegetable oil and soap rather than solvents. Glass plates should have their edges taped or filed.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: plexiglass, glass with taped edges, acetate, laminated card, a baking sheet, a sealed cardboard sheet, a gel plate, or a smooth tabletop wiped clean.

Presses: the palm of the hand, a wooden spoon, a clean brayer, a tennis ball, or an etching press for large or rich prints.

Inks: water-based block printing ink, tempera with a little glycerin to slow drying, acrylic with a retarder, watercolor and water-soluble crayon on the plate printed onto damp paper, and oil-based ink. Paper masks, leaves, and string laid on the plate before printing act as stencils.

PETG plastic sheet is a good all-purpose monotype plate, and plastic and countertop scraps from sign shops and kitchen fitters work too. The back of a used metal plate also works. A smooth doorknob burnishes well by hand.
