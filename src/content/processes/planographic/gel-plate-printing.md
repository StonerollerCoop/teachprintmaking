## How it works

A gel plate is a soft, flexible slab, either a commercial plate made of a durable gel or a homemade one cast from gelatin and glycerin. Paint or ink is rolled thinly over it. Leaves, string, lace, bubble wrap, paper cutouts, or a texture tool pressed into the ink lift or mask parts of it, and paper pressed onto the plate by hand picks up the image.

The magic of the gel plate is layering. Each print can be printed again with a new color, and paint dries quickly on paper, so students can build up translucent layers, positive and negative shapes, and ghost prints from leaves that have already been printed once. A leaf pulled away from the plate leaves a negative print; the leaf itself, now covered in paint, can be printed face down for a positive.

A few things are worth knowing. Rolling the brayer slowly lays ink down, and rolling it fast lifts ink back up. Because the plate is transparent, it can be lifted and pressed again over any spot that didn't print, and a sketch or registration marks can sit underneath it. A glossy magazine page pressed onto a freshly inked plate and peeled away lifts some of its printed image into the paint, which then transfers to the next print. <cite>Medley, ch. 3</cite>

For a print built in layers, Gill Thompson fixes the top edge of the paper with a strip of masking tape. The tape works as a hinge, so the paper can be folded back while a new layer is rolled and laid down again in exactly the same place. <cite>Thompson, pp. 37, 41</cite> Her leaf sequences show a useful habit: after the leaves are lifted, their imprint is left in the paint on the plate, and it can be left to dry for five to ten minutes and then lifted with a pale layer as a second, detailed print. <cite>Thompson, pp. 34–36</cite> Roll spare paint off the brayer onto scrap paper between layers, which keeps it from caking and makes good collage paper, but don't use newspaper for this, because its print transfers to the plate. <cite>Thompson, p. 34</cite>

Store a commercial plate between its acetate sheets with every air bubble smoothed out, since trapped bubbles can leave permanent dents, and keep stored plates on edge or with nothing stacked on top. <cite>Thompson, p. 13</cite>

## Making a plate

Homemade plates are made from powdered gelatin, glycerin, and boiling water. Recipes vary a great deal: some use more gelatin for a firmer plate, some add cold water or rubbing alcohol, and some leave out glycerin altogether. A typical plate uses about as much glycerin as boiling water, with the gelatin bloomed in glycerin or cold water first. Widely shared versions include recipes from the printmaker Linda Germain and from Suzanne McNeill's book Gelli Printing. <cite>The Art Museum; Oates</cite>

The steps are much the same in every version. Level the pan first. Stir slowly and gently, since fast stirring fills the mix with bubbles that print as holes. Pour into a smooth, flat pan, drag a strip of newsprint across the surface to skim off foam, and leave the plate undisturbed to set for a few hours, then chill it to make it easier to unmold. A finished plate is usually about a third of an inch thick. <cite>Herringe; Comfortable Shoes Studio</cite>

A damaged plate doesn't have to be thrown away. Cut it into pieces, melt it again in short bursts in a microwave, stirring between them, strain out bits of paint if needed, and pour it again. Cookie cutters can cut a plate into shapes that print as stamps. <cite>Oates; Herringe; Medley, ch. 2</cite>

## History

The gelatin hectograph of the 1870s copied handwritten pages from a tray of gelatin that absorbed special ink, and schools used hectographs to copy worksheets well into the twentieth century. Commercial gel plates, which keep at room temperature and don't spoil, made the method popular in the early twenty-first century with artists, crafters, and schools.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Gel plates suit students from kindergarten up. Acrylic paint and water-based ink clean up with water or baby wipes, and baby oil lifts stains. Keep sharp tools and ballpoint pens away from the plate, since a cut or dent prints in every image afterward.

Making a plate involves boiling water and a hot microwave dish, so an adult should do the cooking. Recipes that add rubbing alcohol produce flammable vapor while hot; heat them only in a microwave, never near a flame, and air the room.

How long a homemade plate lasts depends on the recipe. Plates with plenty of glycerin can keep for months at room temperature, and some printmakers report keeping one for over a year. Plates made with little or no glycerin soften at room temperature, need to be kept cool, and can grow mold. Throw away any plate that does. <cite>Herringe; The Art Museum</cite>

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: commercial gel plates, or homemade plates of gelatin and glycerin cast in a baking pan. Store a plate flat between two sheets of acetate, or in an airtight container, with nothing trapped against the surface, since anything pressed into it leaves a permanent dent.

Presses: the hand. Rub the back of the paper with the palm or fingertips; a clean brayer or wooden spoon gives a more even print.

Inks: acrylic paint, water-based block printing ink, and tempera. Open acrylics stay workable longer. Leaves, lace, sequin waste, netting, string, paper snowflakes, stencils cut from copy paper, and textured rubbing plates make the marks. A few drops of baby oil spritzed onto the inked plate break the paint into painterly spots. <cite>Medley, ch. 3</cite>
