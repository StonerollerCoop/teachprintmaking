## How it works

A screen is a frame with fine mesh stretched tightly across it. The stencil sits on the mesh, blocking the areas that shouldn't print. Ink is laid along one end of the screen and pulled across with a squeegee, which pushes it through the open mesh onto the paper or fabric below. Because the mesh holds every part of the stencil in place, there is no need for bridges, and letters print cleanly.

There are many ways to make the stencil:

- **Paper stencils.** Copy paper or freezer paper cut with scissors and laid under the screen sticks to the mesh with the first pull of ink. It is the fastest way to start.
- **Drawing fluid and screen filler.** The printmaker paints the image on the mesh with drawing fluid, coats the screen with filler, and washes the fluid out, leaving the painted marks open.
- **Crayon and oil pastel.** Drawn directly on the mesh, they block it and print as a rubbing-like texture. Rubbing a crayon over mesh laid on a leaf or burlap picks up the texture.
- **Photo emulsion.** The screen is coated with light-sensitive emulsion and exposed through a transparency. The emulsion hardens where light reaches it and washes out where the image blocked the light. This is how most professional screenprints are made, from hand-drawn or digital artwork.

Each color needs its own stencil, registered with marks or tabs on the printing board. A reduction print can be made from one screen by blocking out more of the mesh after each color.

## History

Stencils on a mesh or with fine connecting threads have deep roots, including Japanese katagami. Samuel Simon of Manchester patented a process using silk stretched on a frame in 1907, and screen printing spread through sign shops and textile works. In the 1930s, artists in the New Deal Federal Art Project, including Anthony Velonis, adapted it for posters and fine art, and the curator Carl Zigrosser helped coin the word serigraph to set artists' screenprints apart from commercial work. In the 1960s, Andy Warhol used photographic screens to repeat images from advertising and the news, and Corita Kent made joyful, text-filled serigraphs in Los Angeles.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Screenprinting with paper stencils works from upper elementary, with an adult helping to pull the squeegee for an even print. Middle school students can manage their own screens. Photo emulsion suits high school and up.

Use water-based screen inks, which clean up with water before they dry. Clean screens promptly, since dried ink clogs the mesh. Photo emulsions, their sensitizers, and emulsion removers can irritate skin and eyes; use gloves, eye protection, and good ventilation, and follow the supplier's instructions. Older books describe lacquer stencils and solvent-based inks, which belong only in professionally ventilated studios.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Screens: commercial aluminum or wood screens, or a homemade screen of mesh stapled tightly to a picture frame or a set of canvas stretchers. A finer mesh gives sharper detail; a coarser mesh lays down more ink for fabric.

Stencils: copy paper, freezer paper, contact paper, drawing fluid and screen filler, crayon, oil pastel, and photo emulsion.

Tools: a squeegee slightly wider than the image, a hinged printing board, and packing tape to seal the edges of the screen.

Inks: water-based screen ink for paper, textile ink for fabric (heat set with an iron), or acrylic paint mixed with screen-printing medium so it doesn't dry in the mesh.
