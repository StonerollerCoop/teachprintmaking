## How it works

A stencil duplicator makes many copies from a stencil wrapped around an inked drum. The mimeograph used a waxed stencil sheet, cut by the keys of a typewriter or drawn with a stylus. Ink pressed through the cuts onto each sheet of paper as the drum turned. A mimeograph stencil cut on a typewriter can also be taped to the underside of a silk screen and printed by hand, which puts typed words into a screenprint. Albert Kosloff wrote a booklet on screenprinting with mimeograph-type stencils. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 230–231 and 233</cite> Silvie Turner made the same point in 1983 about Gestetner and Roneo duplicating stencils, and she found that lace doilies and punched computer tape make some of the simplest screen stencils of all. <cite>Turner 1983, p. 100</cite>

The Risograph works the same way with modern tools. The machine scans or receives an image and burns tiny holes into a master stencil with heat, wraps the master around a drum of ink, and prints one color at a time. Each color is a separate drum, and each color layer is a separate black-and-white image. Students designing for Riso draw or prepare one layer for each color, as in screenprinting or pochoir, and see how overlapping translucent inks mix.

Riso inks are bright, translucent, and soy-based. Registration between layers drifts slightly, and the resulting imperfection is a large part of the look artists love.

## History

Thomas Edison's stencil patents in the 1870s and 1880s led to the mimeograph, sold by the A. B. Dick Company. For nearly a century, schools, churches, clubs, and activist groups printed newsletters, worksheets, and pamphlets on mimeographs. The Japanese company Riso Kagaku released the Risograph in 1986 as a fast, economical duplicator for offices and schools. Artists, designers, and zine makers later adopted it, and Riso studios now run in many cities and design schools.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Students from middle school up can design color separations by hand or digitally. The machine itself is run by the teacher or a studio technician. Riso inks are soy-based and low in odor, and the machines need no special ventilation.

Schools without a Riso can teach the same thinking with layered stencils or screenprinting, or by sending student separations to a community Riso studio.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Masters: thermal stencils made inside the machine. Separations can be drawn on paper in black marker, pencil, or brush, or prepared digitally as grayscale files, one per color.

Presses: the duplicator itself.

Inks: Riso drum colors, which come in dozens of shades, including fluorescent pink and fluorescent orange. Uncoated paper absorbs the ink best; coated papers smudge.
