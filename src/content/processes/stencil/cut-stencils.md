## How it works

A stencil is a thin sheet with openings in it. Color dabbed, rolled, or sprayed over the stencil reaches the paper only through the openings. The shape that was cut out can be used too, as a mask: lay it on the paper, color around it, and lift it to leave its silhouette blank.

Any flat object can be a mask. A hand, a leaf, a key, a doily, or a strip of tape blocks color and leaves its outline behind. Spattering paint with a toothbrush over a leaf gives a soft halo; dabbing with a sponge gives a crisp edge.

A cut stencil has one rule students discover quickly: a shape floating inside another shape falls out. The center of a letter O or the eye of a face needs a bridge, a thin strip that connects it to the rest of the stencil. Designing with bridges is a lesson in negative space, and stencil lettering is built around them.

## History

The oldest prints we know are stencils. A hand stencil on a cave wall in Sulawesi is at least 67,800 years old; red hand stencils line the cave of El Castillo in Spain; and hundreds of overlapping hands cover the Cave of the Hands in Argentina. People appear to have discovered the stencil independently all over the world, by blowing pigment through a tube or from the mouth.

Japanese craftspeople cut katagami stencils from layers of mulberry paper bonded with persimmon tannin, holding loose pieces in place with fine threads, to print rice-paste resist on kimono cloth. Fijian makers cut stencils from banana and pandanus leaves to pattern masi barkcloth, a practice documented since at least the 1840s.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Hand stencils, leaf masks, and sponge printing through simple shapes suit preschool and up. Scissor-cut stencils suit the early grades. Craft knives suit middle school and up, with a cutting mat, a metal ruler, and the rule that the free hand always stays behind the blade.

Spray paint and airbrush stencils should only be done outdoors or in a spray booth, with a respirator rated for paint vapor. Toothbrush spatter with watercolor or tempera is the safe classroom version.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Stencils: copy paper, freezer paper (which irons onto fabric and peels off cleanly), cardstock, file folders, acetate, contact paper, painter's tape, leaves, doilies, lace, netting, and hands.

Tools: sponges, stencil brushes, foam daubers, brayers, toothbrushes for spatter, and spray bottles of thin paint.

Inks: tempera, acrylic paint, watercolor, water-based block printing ink, and fabric paint for cloth.
