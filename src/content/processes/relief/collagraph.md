## How it works

A collagraph plate is a collage. Shapes of card, fabric, lace, string, sandpaper, leaves, tape, or sand are glued to a stiff board, and the plate is sealed with shellac, acrylic medium, or diluted glue so it won't soak up the ink.

A collagraph can be printed two ways, which is why it sits between relief and intaglio. Rolled with a brayer, the ink catches only the highest surfaces, and it prints like a relief block with no press needed. Inked with a stiff brush, then wiped with tarlatan and paper so ink stays in the low areas and textured surfaces, it prints like an intaglio plate, with rich darks and soft edges. Intaglio inking needs a press and damp paper.

Students quickly learn which materials print dark and which print light. Rough textures like sandpaper and carborundum grit hold a lot of ink; smooth tape and glossy card hold very little. Gesso brushed or scraped on adds marks of its own.

Absorbent materials such as string, fabric, and card soak up the first rolls of ink, so a new plate may need several inkings before it prints at full strength. <cite>Martin, pp. 55–57</cite>

Charles Smith printed collages without inking them at all. Ink is rolled onto a smooth, uncut block, paper is laid on the ink, and an uninked collage goes face down on top of the paper. Under pressure the raised parts of the collage push the paper harder against the ink, so they print darker. Heller calls this Smith's makeready technique, after the printer's word for packing placed under weak areas of a block. Smith wrote it up in his book Experiments in Relief Print Making (1954). <cite>Heller 1958, p. 97</cite>

## History

The printmaker Glen Alps coined the word collagraph in the 1950s, from collage and graph. <cite>Ross and Romano 1974; Fick and Grabowski, ch. 6</cite> Its roots go back further: the Norwegian artist Rolf Nesch built printing plates from soldered and assembled pieces of metal in the 1930s. John Ross, Clare Romano, and others developed cardboard and gesso collagraphs as a teaching method in the following decades. <cite>Ross, Romano, and Ross 1990, ch. 3</cite>

<span class="flag">The French artist Pierre Roche is also named as a forerunner.</span> <cite>Hughes and Vernon-Morris, Intaglio</cite>

Heller credits Boris Margo with inventing the cellocut, a plate built up from celluloid dissolved in acetone. Celluloid is a fire hazard and acetone fumes need strong ventilation, so printmakers today build similar raised surfaces from acrylic gel and modeling paste. <cite>Heller 1958, pp. 248–249</cite>

## Ages, safety, and setting

Relief-inked collagraphs suit young children; a K–2 class can glue cardboard shapes and texture scraps onto board and print them by hand. Intaglio-inked collagraphs suit upper elementary and up, wherever a press is available.

Seal plates with acrylic medium, PVA glue, or shellac thinned with alcohol. Avoid the lacquer and solvent-based sealers in older manuals. Sand and grit glued to a plate should be fully sealed so they don't shed.

Beans, string, sandpaper, and raised glue lines can be felt before and after printing, which makes the collagraph plate one of the best for students who are blind or have low vision. <cite>Martin, pp. 54–59; Heller 1958, p. 97</cite>

Clean collagraph plates with vegetable oil or a citrus cleaner. Alcohol and acetone dissolve shellac and some sealers, and a plate cleaned with them can come apart. Never warm a collagraph on a hotplate. <cite>Fick and Grabowski, ch. 6</cite>

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: chipboard, mat board, cereal-box card, and hardboard, built up with card, corrugated cardboard, doilies, lace, rickrack, string, sandpaper, tissue, leaves, fabric, coffee grounds, sand, rice and beans, embossed greeting cards, and gesso. Coins pressed into damp mat board leave a crisp low relief. Heller's collage blocks also used cheesecloth, wire screen, pipe cleaners, paper clips, and barley set into wet glue, and Martin adds buttons, pasta, and dried peas. John Elliot cuts mat board blocks to different levels and inks them with soft oil pastels in place of printing ink. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 97; Martin, pp. 55 and 145</cite>

Presses: hand, spoon, or rolling pin for relief inking; an etching press, pasta machine, or old clothes wringer for intaglio inking.

Inks: water-based relief ink for surface printing; water-washable intaglio ink for wiped plates.

Seal both sides of the plate, with two or three thin coats, so it doesn't curl or soak up ink. Thick objects such as coins and keys print better set into a hollow cut in the board, so the plate stays fairly level. Carborundum grit mixed into acrylic medium or wood glue and brushed on gives a rich, dark tone when inked as intaglio. An old toothbrush or a felt dauber works ink into deep textures, and heavier, damp paper of about 250 grams or more picks up the detail. <cite>Hughes and Vernon-Morris, Intaglio; Fick and Grabowski, ch. 6</cite>
