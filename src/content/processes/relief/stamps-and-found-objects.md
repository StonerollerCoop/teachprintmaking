## How it works

Anything with a surface that can hold ink can print. A potato cut in half, a cork, a bottle cap, the end of a dowel, a sponge, or a fingertip can be pressed into ink and stamped onto paper. Cut a shape into the flat face of a potato or a gum eraser and the uncut part becomes a stamp. <cite>Kafka, ch. II; DeSherlia, sec. IV</cite>

Foam takes the idea one step further. Drawing into a sheet of craft foam or a clean polystyrene tray with a dull pencil presses a line below the surface, and that line stays white when the sheet is rolled with ink. It is the gentlest way to teach the logic of relief: what is pressed down stays blank, and what stands up prints.

String glued around a cardboard tube or a can becomes a roller. String glued to a block, or a design trailed in white glue and left to dry, prints as raised line. Leaves, feathers, and fish print their own surface; the Japanese practice of printing fish, gyotaku, began as a way for anglers to record a catch. <cite>DeSherlia, sec. IV</cite>

An inked object can also be printed by offset. Roll a clean brayer over an inked leaf, toy, or textured scrap so the image transfers to the roller, then roll the brayer across paper. Objects that can't be held, such as fabric scraps and lace, can be laid on the inked slab, covered with scrap paper, and rolled, then lifted and printed. <cite>Medley, chs. 2–3</cite>

## History

Carved cylinder seals rolled across clay in Mesopotamia more than five thousand years ago are stamps. Tahitian makers stamped barkcloth with the cut ends of bamboo. Tongan makers rub dye over barkcloth laid on design tablets built from coconut-leaf midribs, a kind of rubbing over a raised surface. Asante artisans in Ghana carve adinkra stamps from calabash gourd. Rubbings from carved stone, made in China by the 600s, are the same principle children use with a crayon over a coin.

## Ages, safety, and setting

This is printing for the youngest students. Preschoolers can print with sponges, fingers, and objects. An adult should cut vegetables and any eraser or potato designs for children too young to handle a knife, or use a plastic knife on a soft potato for children who can. Foam drawing with a pencil suits kindergarten and up. Gyotaku works well from the early grades; use a fish from the market, wash hands afterward, and keep printing tools away from food. <cite>Ross, Romano, and Ross 1990, "School Printmaking"</cite>

Tempera and washable paint work for most of this. Stamp pads suit small objects and erasers.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: potatoes, carrots, celery ends, apples, onions, corks, spools, bottle caps, Lego bricks, clothespins, dowels, sponges, bubble wrap, foam trays, craft foam, gum erasers, string glued to card or tubes, dried glue lines, leaves, and fish.

Presses: the hand, a fist, a clean brayer rolled over the back of the paper, a rolling pin, or the back of a wooden spoon.

Inks: tempera, washable paint, water-based block printing ink, stamp pads, and watercolor brushed onto leaves.

Wood and cork take ink well from a brayer; rubber and foam stamps do better pressed onto a stamp pad or a thin layer of ink on a slab. Cut citrus and cucumber print more cleanly after drying cut side up for about an hour, while lettuce and other leafy greens wilt and need to be printed right away. A slice off the end refreshes a potato or carrot stamp the next day. Wear gloves or skip hot peppers, since the oils burn skin. <cite>Medley, chs. 2–3</cite>
