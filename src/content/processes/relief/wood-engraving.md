## How it works

A wood engraving is cut into the end grain of a block, the surface you see when a log is sawn across. End grain has no direction to split along, so it can be cut cleanly in any direction with very fine lines. The tools are engravers' gravers and spitstickers, pushed through the wood rather than levered like a gouge. The block usually rests on a leather sandbag so it can be turned into the cut.

Because the lines are so fine, wood engravings are often worked as white line on black: the engraver cuts light into a dark field, and the print reads as a drawing made of light. The blocks are traditionally made type high, the same height as metal type, so text and image can print together on a letterpress.

## History

Thomas Bewick, working in Newcastle in the 1790s, cut detailed images of animals into boxwood end grain. His History of British Birds became a forerunner of modern field guides, and wood engraving became the standard way to illustrate books and newspapers for most of the nineteenth century, until photographic methods replaced it. Artists revived it in the twentieth century as a fine art.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Wood engraving is a high school, college, and adult process. The tools are small and very sharp and are pushed toward the work, so students need instruction in holding the graver with the hand braced and the free hand clear of the line of cut. A sandbag steadies the block and lets the engraver turn it instead of turning the tool.

Boxwood is expensive and scarce. Resin engraving blocks and end-grain blocks of maple or lemonwood are good substitutes for learning.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: end-grain boxwood, maple, lemonwood, and resin engraving blocks. Some teachers practice line technique on plexiglass or scratchboard first.

Presses: a baren or burnishing spoon for small blocks, or a proof press or letterpress, since the blocks are type high.

Inks: oil-based relief ink rolled very thin, so the fine lines don't fill in.
