## How it works

Letterpress is relief printing from type. Each letter is a separate raised piece, cast in metal or cut in wood, made so that its face is backward. Letters are set one at a time into a composing stick, spaced with blank pieces of metal, and locked into a frame called a chase. Inked and pressed into paper, the backward letters print forward.

Setting type by hand makes typography physical. Students hold the space between words in their fingers as a piece of metal. Leading, the space between lines, gets its name from the strips of lead set between rows of type. Every letter sits on the same baseline because every piece of type is the same height, 0.918 inch in the United States, called type high.

Students don't need a case of type to learn this. Letters carved backward in foam, soft-cut blocks, or linoleum, or cut from craft foam and glued to a block, teach the same lessons about reversal, spacing, and the baseline. A linocut mounted type high can be printed in the same chase as type.

Type height is 0.918 inch in the United States and Britain, and slightly taller, 0.928 inch, in continental Europe. A carved block mounted on plywood usually needs a layer of card or board underneath to reach type height.

## History

The Phaistos Disc from Minoan Crete, pressed with one stamp per sign, may be the oldest text made with reusable signs. In China, Bi Sheng made movable type from baked clay around 1040, and the oldest surviving book printed with cast metal type, Jikji, was printed in Korea in 1377. Johannes Gutenberg combined metal type, a screw press, and an oil-based ink in Mainz around 1450. Aldus Manutius introduced italic type and the small pocket book in 1501, and William Caslon's types became the typefaces of colonial American printing.

In the nineteenth century, mass-produced wood type made the loud mixed-letter posters of the period. The Linotype machine took hand-setting out of newspapers in the 1880s. Vandercook proof presses, built for checking type, became the backbone of art school and community print shops once commercial printing moved on.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Carved and cut letters suit upper elementary students and up. Mirror writing is a good warm-up: students check reversed letters in a hand mirror before carving.

Metal type is made of a lead alloy. Students handling it should not eat or drink in the shop and should wash their hands afterward, and younger children should work with wood type, polymer plates, or carved blocks instead. Presses have pinch points; tabletop and proof presses need a demonstration and supervision.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: metal and wood type, carved linoleum or foam letters, letters cut from craft foam or cardboard, and photopolymer plates made from digital files.

Presses: a baren or spoon for carved blocks, a tabletop platen press, a proof press such as a Vandercook, or an etching press with a type-high bed. Uneven impressions are corrected with makeready, thin layers of paper placed under low areas.

Inks: oil-based or rubber-based letterpress inks rolled very thin, and water-based relief inks for carved letters.
