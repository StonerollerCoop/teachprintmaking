## How it works

The printmaker draws directly into the plate with a sharp, hard point. The point pushes up a ridge of rough material along each line, called the burr. When the plate is inked and wiped, ink collects in the groove and catches in the burr, and the printed line has a soft, velvety edge no other method gives.

Pressure controls the line. A light scratch prints pale and fine; a heavy one prints dark and wide. Crosshatching builds tone, and sandpaper or a wire brush rubbed across the plate makes larger gray areas.

The burr wears down under the pressure of the press, so a drypoint on soft material gives only a small number of strong prints. That suits a classroom, where each student usually wants a few good impressions.

Lay an early and a late impression side by side, and students can see the burr wear away. <cite>Martin, p. 78</cite> On metal, a sketch can be transferred by dampening a pencil or ballpoint drawing and running it face down through the press on the plate, which leaves a faint guide to scratch over. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 165</cite>

Drypoint on a juice box or the inside of a food carton adds another possibility. The plastic coating can be scratched, and it can also be cut and peeled away to expose the cardboard underneath. The bare card holds ink like a sponge and prints as a solid dark. <cite>Dalton, ch. 4</cite>

On soft plates the burr wears quickly. <span class="flag">Copper and zinc give perhaps twenty strong impressions; plastic and card give fewer.</span> <cite>Hughes and Vernon-Morris, Intaglio</cite> Francis Tinsley puts the figure for bare copper at ten to twelve prints, and Martin's demonstration plate gave about ten. <cite>Tinsley 1983, p. 53; Martin, p. 78</cite> A carbide-tipped point stays sharp far longer than a steel needle. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 113</cite> Sandpaper and steel wool scratched into a smooth whiteboard sheet make texture that holds ink. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 144</cite>

Heavy-handed inking and wiping wears a drypoint down early. Printers also tend to wipe a drypoint too clean, but the ink is meant to sit in the burr along the line, so the print should keep a soft, furry edge. A drypoint also needs less press pressure than an etching. On acrylic sheet, Tinsley thins the ink with a little thin oil so it inks and wipes more easily. <cite>Tinsley 1983, pp. 51–53</cite>

Clear plastic has a practical advantage for beginners: it can be laid straight over a drawing and the lines followed through it. Kim Major-George prefers polypropylene sheet for this, finds drypoint card too thin and easily torn, and mounts acetate on board before working it. A nail or long screw pushed through a cork or a wooden holder makes a cheap scribe. <cite>Major-George, p. 44</cite> Like any drypoint point, it is sharp, so keep it for students old enough to work with one safely. Grabowski and Fick list high-impact polystyrene and clear acrylic as good scratching plates. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 142</cite>

## History

Drypoint lines appear in prints from the late fifteenth century. Rembrandt combined drypoint with etching and reworked his plates repeatedly, so his impressions vary from one printing to the next. In schools, drypoint on plastic became popular because it needs no chemicals at all. In 1958 Heller dismissed celluloid drypoint as useless even for practice, and plastic plates have since become the standard classroom method. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 165</cite> In recent years, printmakers have shared methods for drypoint on flattened juice and milk cartons, foil cake tins, and plastic-coated card.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Juice-box drypoint, with a blunt scribe or an old ballpoint pen and a press run by the teacher, works from upper elementary. Acetate and plexiglass drypoint with a proper scribe suits middle school and up. Scribes are sharp; keep the free hand away from the line of the stroke and store scribes point-down in a block.

Drypoint needs no chemicals. Water-washable intaglio ink cleans up with soap and water.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: rigid acetate, PETG, plexiglass, flattened juice and milk cartons, plastic-lined food cartons, the lid of a foil cake tin, plastic-coated card, and copper or zinc for older students. Grey board sealed with several coats of hard wax oil makes a homemade drypoint card, a method shared by the printmaker Mary Dalton. <cite>Dalton, ch. 10</cite>

Presses: an etching press is ideal. A pasta machine with felt and a plexiglass carrier, an old clothes wringer for shallow lines, or a small 3D-printed press can work at small sizes. Hand burnishing rarely pushes damp paper far enough into the lines, though a firm spoon on thin damp paper gives a pale proof.

Inks: water-washable intaglio ink, wiped with tarlatan and then with paper or the palm. Paper should be soaked or misted and blotted before printing. Zinc is easier to scratch than copper but wears faster. Martin wipes metal drypoint plates with paper instead of the hand, because the burr is abrasive. <cite>Martin, pp. 77 and 105</cite>
