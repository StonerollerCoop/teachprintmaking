## How it works

An engraver holds a burin, a steel bar with a diamond-shaped point, with the handle cupped in the palm and pushes it through the metal. The burin lifts a curl of metal out of the plate and leaves a clean V-shaped groove. Pushing harder or tilting the tool swells the line; easing off tapers it. Curves are cut by turning the plate on a leather sandbag while the hand stays steady.

The burr that drypoint keeps is scraped off in engraving, so the printed line is crisp and hard-edged. Tone is built with parallel lines, crosshatching, and dots, and the engraver's systems of lines can model forms with astonishing precision.

## History

Goldsmiths, who already decorated metal with cut lines, began inking engraved plates and printing them on paper in the 1430s. Martin Schongauer and Albrecht Dürer brought engraving to great refinement in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. For centuries afterward, engraving was the way paintings, maps, and banknotes were reproduced, and engravers on banknotes and stamps still use the burin today.

## Ages, safety, and setting

Engraving is slow and demanding and suits high school, college, and adult students who have already tried drypoint. The burin is very sharp and is pushed forward with force, so the hand holding the plate must stay behind and to the side of the cut. A sandbag keeps the plate steady.

Engraving needs no chemicals. Metal shavings are sharp; sweep them up rather than brushing them with a hand.

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: copper is the classic metal. Plexiglass engraves well for practice, and zinc, brass, and aluminum can also be used.

Presses: an etching press. Engraved lines are shallow and crisp, so a small press with good pressure works well.

Inks: water-washable or oil-based intaglio ink, wiped with tarlatan and polished with paper or the palm, printed on damp paper.
