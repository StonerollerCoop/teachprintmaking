## How it works

A metal plate is coated with a ground, a thin layer that resists the etchant. The printmaker draws through the ground with a needle, exposing bare metal wherever a line should print. The plate goes into an etching bath, which eats into the exposed metal and leaves grooves. The longer the bite, the deeper and darker the line. Lines can be protected with stop-out varnish partway through, so one plate holds lines of several depths.

A hard ground takes needle drawing. A soft ground stays tacky, so anything pressed into it lifts the ground away: a drawing made on paper laid over the plate, or the texture of lace, leaves, or fabric. The plate is then cleaned, inked, wiped, and printed on damp paper through a press.

Soft ground takes almost any texture pressed into it: fabric, tarlatan, cellophane, ribbon, feathers, leaves, wood veneer, a fingerprint, or the teeth of a pocket comb. <cite>Martin, pp. 96–97; Heller 1958, pp. 117 and 157</cite>

Etching feels like drawing. The needle moves freely through the ground, with none of the resistance of a burin, and that freedom drew painters to the process for five centuries.

<span class="flag">Heller describes an offset-roller color method from Stanley William Hayter's Atelier 17, in which color is rolled over the surface of an inked and wiped plate so one plate prints several colors in a single pass.</span> <cite>Heller 1958, p. 189</cite>

## History

Armorers decorated steel with acid long before anyone printed from it. Daniel Hopfer, an armor decorator in Augsburg, is widely believed to have been among the first to print from an etched plate around 1500, and the earliest dated etchings come from the 1510s. Rembrandt made etching a painter's medium. Jacques Callot developed the échoppe, a needle with an angled face for swelling lines. Francisco Goya combined etching and aquatint in his print series.

Traditional etching used nitric acid and solvent-based grounds. From the 1990s, printmakers developed safer methods. Friedhard Kiekeben developed Edinburgh Etch, a ferric chloride solution with citric acid for copper. Copper sulfate and salt solutions etch zinc, aluminum, and steel without nitric acid. Keith Howard developed intaglio methods using photopolymer film, and Dan Welden developed Solarplate, a light-sensitive plate developed in water. <cite>Adams; Fick and Grabowski, ch. 5</cite> Grabowski and Fick's first edition describes Kiekeben's Edinburgh Etch, Howard's photopolymer film, and Welden's Solarplate. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, pp. 107–108, 128, 130</cite>

Kiekeben also developed Saline Sulfate Etch, an improved copper sulfate and salt etch for zinc, alongside Cedric Green's copper sulfate etch. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 108</cite> <span class="flag">It also etches aluminum and steel.</span> <cite>Fick and Grabowski, ch. 5</cite> <span class="flag">Andrew Baldwin developed BIG, a hard ground rolled onto the plate and baked, and Dwight Pogue and Skip Klepacki developed plant-based cleaners to replace studio solvents.</span> <cite>Fick and Grabowski, ch. 5 and suppliers</cite>

Copper sulfate breaks through a ground faster than other etches, so grounds need to be laid a little thicker. Handle the powder with the fans off so it doesn't lift into the air, wear a dust mask and gloves when mixing a new bath, and wipe up drips before they dry to a harmful residue. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 108</cite>

## Ages, safety, and setting

Etching belongs in high school, college, and adult classes, and only with the safer methods. Older manuals describe practices that should not be used anywhere students are present, including nitric acid baths without fume extraction, grounds made with chloroform or ether, smoking grounds over a flame, dichromate etches, and asbestos drying boards. <cite>Ross and Romano 1974</cite> If a school can't provide a sink, ventilation, and a plan for disposing of spent etchant, drypoint and collagraph teach the same printing skills without any of it.

Martin and Heller both etch with Dutch mordant, a mix of hydrochloric acid and potassium chlorate that gives off chlorine gas, and neither book's acid strengths should be followed. Dutch mordant has no place in a school. <cite>Martin, p. 92; Heller 1958, p. 130</cite>

With the safer methods, students still need nitrile gloves, goggles, and an apron. Ferric chloride stains and irritates skin, and copper sulfate is toxic to aquatic life, so neither goes down the drain. Follow the supplier's safety data sheet and local disposal rules.

<span class="flag">Plates can be degreased with a paste of salt and vinegar</span> in place of the old whiting and ammonia; Grabowski and Fick avoid ammonia and use a bleach-free scouring powder. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 110</cite> The hazard in many inks is the pigment, not the oil; check labels for the AP seal of the Art and Creative Materials Institute, which marks products certified nontoxic, and keep the safety data sheet for every chemical in the room. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, pp. 22, 32–33</cite>

## Plates, presses, and inks

Plates: copper with ferric chloride or Edinburgh Etch; zinc, aluminum, or steel with copper sulfate and salt; photopolymer plates and films exposed to light and developed in water.

Grounds: acrylic hard and soft grounds that clean up with soda ash or a mild alkaline solution; contact paper or packing tape as a resist for large areas.

Presses: an etching press. Small tabletop presses work well for plates up to about the size of a postcard.

Inks: water-washable intaglio ink, wiped with tarlatan, printed on soaked and blotted paper.

An etching needle is easy to improvise. Heller used a darning needle, a dulled dental tool, or an old phonograph needle held in a pin vise. Martin draws double lines with a split-nib pen and dotted lines with a dentist's drill bit used as a roulette. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 129; Martin, p. 91</cite>

Copper roofing sheet is an inexpensive alternative to etcher's copper. Zinc etches fast but wears and shifts some ink colors. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 106</cite> Tinsley notes that copper, and zinc even more, can react with some pigments, so white prints grey and yellow turns green, while earth colors such as browns and ochres stay stable. It is a small lesson in chemistry meeting color. <cite>Tinsley 1983, p. 41</cite> Steel doesn't react with pigments this way. <cite>Tinsley 1983, p. 41; Hughes and Vernon-Morris, Intaglio</cite>

Photopolymer film and plates need a positive, and it doesn't have to come from a computer. A drawing in opaque ink on clear drafting film works. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 128</cite> Tinsley's photo-etching chapter of 1983 already follows the same order a photopolymer class uses today: positive, contact, ultraviolet exposure, development, and a test strip. <cite>Tinsley 1983, pp. 59–61</cite> Some students react to the acrylates in photopolymer, so give them nitrile gloves. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 131</cite>
