The power of printmaking is repetition. A carved block can print the same image dozens or hundreds of times, which is why prints spread ideas, prayers, posters, and news long before photography. An edition is the set of matching prints an artist decides to make from one matrix.

## How an edition is marked

Printmakers sign each print in pencil in the margin below the image. Most write the edition number on the left as a fraction, so 3/20 means the third print of an edition of twenty. The title often goes in the middle, and the signature on the right. Prints made outside the edition are marked too: A/P for an artist's proof, or "trial proof" for a print made while the work was still changing.

Before the edition is printed, the artist chooses one proof as the standard every print will be matched against. It is marked BAT, for bon à tirer, French for "good to pull." Artist's proofs are prints identical to the edition that the artist keeps, and by convention they number no more than about a tenth of the edition. Prints that differ from the edition are trial proofs or state proofs, not artist's proofs. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 31</cite>

A few other conventions are worth teaching, and all of them can be broken for a reason. Printmaking paper is usually torn to size rather than cut, with any deckle edge at the right or bottom. The margins are equal at the top and sides and a little deeper at the bottom. The back of the print should be as clean as the front. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 31</cite>

Some prints can't be editioned at all. A monotype is always an edition of one. A reduction print fixes its edition size at the start, because the block is cut away a little more for every color and can never print the earlier stages again. Rembrandt kept reworking his plates, so his prints exist in several different states.

## Why it matters in the classroom

Asking students to pull three matching prints teaches more than pulling one. They have to ink the same way each time, line the paper up the same way, and press evenly. The differences between their three prints show them exactly what to adjust. Signing and numbering the edition gives them the satisfaction of finishing like a professional.

## Things to notice with students

- Lay out a whole class edition and look for the best print in each student's set. Ask what made it the best.
- Ask whether every print needs to match. Some printmakers, Peter Wray and Mandy Walden among them, let every impression differ or color each one by hand, and say so. <cite>Major-George, pp. 87–88</cite>
- Trade prints. An edition is meant to be shared, and a class print exchange is one of the oldest traditions in printmaking.

## Words to know

**Edition:** the set of matching prints made from one matrix.
**Impression:** a single print.
**Proof:** a test print made before or outside the edition.
**State:** a version of a print made at one stage of work on the plate.
**BAT (bon à tirer):** the approved proof that the edition is matched against.
**Ghost:** a second, paler print taken from the ink left on a plate.
