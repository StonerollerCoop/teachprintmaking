Every print starts with something that carries the image: a block, a plate, a screen, a stencil. Printmakers call it the matrix. Each of the four families of printmaking has its own kind of matrix, and almost anything can become one.

## Relief: a raised surface

The image stands up and takes the ink, and anything cut or pressed away stays the color of the paper. Wood and linoleum are the classic relief blocks. In classrooms, foam sheets, cardboard, erasers, potatoes, and collaged textures all work. Tongan makers build their relief matrix from coconut-leaf ribs sewn to a backing, and rub the cloth over it.

Heller suggested cigar boxes and the ends of orange crates as woodcut blocks, and Martin sends beginners to a lumberyard for pine and plywood offcuts. <cite>Heller 1958, p. 78; Martin, p. 36</cite> A collage block can be built from almost anything glued to card: buttons, pasta, dried peas, rice, yarn, sandpaper, masking tape, cheesecloth, pipe cleaners, paper clips, or wire screen. <cite>Martin, pp. 54–59; Heller 1958, p. 97</cite>

## Intaglio: lines below the surface

The image is cut, scratched, or etched into a plate, and the ink is pushed into those lines and wiped from the surface. Copper and zinc are traditional. Clear acetate, plexiglass, and the inside of a cleaned juice carton all work for drypoint, and old CDs can be scratched too.

## Planographic: a flat surface

Nothing is cut at all. In lithography the image is drawn on stone or a metal plate with something greasy, and chemistry keeps the ink on the drawing. In monotype the ink is simply painted or wiped onto glass, plexiglass, metal, or a gel plate and printed once.

## Stencil: a mask with openings

Ink passes through the open parts and is blocked everywhere else. The first stencil was a human hand. Fijian makers cut stencils from banana and pandanus leaves, Japanese craftspeople cut them from layered mulberry paper, and screen printers attach them to stretched mesh. Paper, leaves, tape, and old X-ray film all make good stencils.

## Things to notice with students

- Every matrix makes a mirror image, except a stencil, which can be flipped over and used either way. Ask students to predict what their print will look like before they pull it.
- Look at a matrix and its print side by side and ask what printed and what didn't.
- Martin prints one subject in more than eight processes so their marks can be compared side by side. A class can do the same with one simple drawing, made as a foam print, a monotype, a stencil, and a drypoint. <cite>Martin, pp. 12–13</cite>

## Words to know

**Matrix:** the block, plate, screen, or stencil that carries the image.
**Plate:** a flat matrix, usually for intaglio or planographic printing.
**Block:** a relief matrix, usually wood or linoleum.
**Reversal:** the mirror-image flip that happens when a block or plate is printed.
