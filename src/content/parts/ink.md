Every printing ink is made of two things: a pigment, which gives the color, and a vehicle or binder, which carries the pigment and makes it stick. Cave painters most likely blew red and yellow earth pigments mixed with water or saliva. Gutenberg's printers used soot ground into a varnish of boiled linseed oil. Japanese woodblock printers brushed watercolor pigment onto the block together with a little rice paste. Asante artisans in Ghana print Adinkra symbols with a dark dye boiled from tree bark.

## Why it matters

The right ink depends on the matrix and the substrate. Relief printing wants a stiff, tacky ink rolled very thin, so it sits on the raised surface without flooding the cuts. Intaglio wants a stiff ink that can be pushed into lines and wiped cleanly from the surface. Screen printing wants a smoother ink that will pass through mesh. Monotype can use almost anything that stays wet long enough to print.

Water-based inks clean up with soap and water and are the usual choice in schools. Oil-based inks stay workable longer, print richer, and need vegetable oil and soap to clean. Tempera and acrylic paint can stand in for ink in many classroom lessons, but they dry faster and print less evenly.

## Listening to the ink

Experienced printers judge ink by sound and feel. Roll it out on a slab and listen: when it is the right thickness it makes a soft hiss, a little like velcro pulling apart. Too much ink sounds sticky and looks shiny and ridged; too little looks dry and patchy. Grabowski and Fick roll until the ink is smooth and velvety and makes a slightly tacky sound, then keep rolling it onto the block until the same sound comes back. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 90</cite>

## Mixing color

Always add the darker color to the lighter one, a little at a time. Clear transparent base counts as a light color. A few transparent colors can make many more where they overlap. <cite>Grabowski and Fick 2009, p. 25</cite>

For an edition, mix enough ink for every print at the start, because matching a color later is hard. Note the proportions and keep a dab of each color on paper. <cite>Tinsley 1983, p. 44</cite> Test a mixed color by dabbing it out and letting it dry, since dried ink is not always the color it looked in the pot. <cite>Turner 1983, p. 108</cite>

## Finding and making ink from the world around you

Color is all around a school, from the kitchen to the schoolyard. Jason Logan, who founded the Toronto Ink Company, makes ink from weeds, kitchen scraps, rust, and city sidewalks, and his book Make Ink shows how far that idea can reach. <cite>Logan</cite>

The basic pattern is the same for almost any plant color. Something full of color is soaked or simmered in water, strained through a sieve and then a coffee filter, and mixed with a little gum arabic, which binds the color to the paper. Kitchen scraps are the safest place to start with young children. Onion skins and turmeric give yellows, coffee and tea give browns, beets give pinks, black beans give a blue, and purple cabbage gives a purple. An adult does any simmering at the stove, and the children can tear the skins and paint with what comes out of the pot. Simmering vinegar gives off sharp fumes, so open a window. <cite>Logan</cite>

Purple cabbage water changes color with acidity. A splash of vinegar turns it pink, and a pinch of baking soda turns it blue or green, so one pot makes several inks and a lesson in acids and bases. Tea, acorn caps, and walnut hulls are rich in tannin, and a little iron darkens them to gray or black. This is the chemistry of the iron gall ink used for medieval manuscripts. For a class, the iron can come from a few rusty nails steeped in vinegar and water by an adult. <cite>Logan</cite>

Logan labels every bottle with the place the color came from and the date it was gathered. Students can do the same on a test card, painting a swatch of each ink with its source, place, and date written beneath it, so a batch of homemade inks becomes a record of where they live. <cite>Logan</cite>

### Printing with homemade ink

Logan makes his inks for pen and brush, and he says almost nothing about printing. They are thin, so they bead up and soak in when rolled onto a relief block. To roll them, the color has to be reduced and thickened, or a concentrated natural ink can tint a clear water-based extender, sometimes sold as transparent base. Homemade inks work nearly as they are for monotype, brushed onto a smooth plate and printed onto damp paper, and they suit stencils, dabbed, sponged, or spattered through the openings. Expect pale, transparent colors that may fade in light. <cite>Logan</cite>

### Safety

- Leave out wintergreen oil, which Logan uses to keep ink from spoiling. It is concentrated methyl salicylate, and a teaspoon can kill a small child. A whole clove in the bottle or a place in the refrigerator does the same job.
- Keep lye and pokeberry away from children entirely. Lye causes severe burns, and every part of the pokeberry plant is poisonous.
- Keep iron supplements and ferrous sulfate locked away from children. Iron tablets look like candy, and iron poisoning is dangerous for young children.
- Check allergy records before using walnuts, acorns, or any other nut.
- Forage only where you have permission, such as the school grounds or a garden. Soil along railway lines and roadsides can carry heavy metals and weed killers, and children should never taste anything they gather.

## Things to notice with students

- Roll out a little too much ink and print, then roll it thinner and print again. Compare.
- Mix two colors and overprint them to see how transparent inks build new colors.
- Grind a little charcoal or soft pastel into a few drops of liquid soap or gum arabic and see whether it will print. Then talk about what a binder does.

## Words to know

**Pigment:** the colored powder in ink or paint.
**Vehicle or binder:** the liquid that carries the pigment and binds it to the surface.
**Tack:** how sticky an ink is.
**Transparent and opaque:** whether a layer of ink lets the color beneath show through.
