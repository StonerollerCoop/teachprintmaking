The substrate is whatever receives the ink. Most of the time it's paper, but the first prints we know of landed on cave walls, and some of the oldest printing traditions in the world are printed on cloth. Tongan and Fijian makers print on barkcloth they beat out of mulberry bark themselves. Students can print on fabric, cardboard, wood, a page of an old book, or the back of last week's worksheet.

## Why it matters

The substrate decides more about a print than beginners expect. A soft, absorbent paper drinks ink up from the block and gives a full, velvety print. A hard, shiny paper lets the ink sit on top and smear. Thin paper picks up detail when it's rubbed by hand, while thick paper needs the pressure of a press to reach into the plate. Fabric moves as you print on it and wants to be pinned or taped down.

Paper also changes with the weather. On a humid day it relaxes and curls; on a dry winter day in a heated classroom it can be stiff and pick up less ink. Printmakers dampen paper on purpose for intaglio printing so it softens enough to be pushed down into the lines of the plate.

## Things to notice with students

- Print the same block on three or four different papers and lay the prints side by side. Ask which one looks best and why.
- Tear a sheet of paper in both directions. It tears more cleanly along the grain, which is the direction its fibers mostly run.
- Hold printmaking paper up to a window and look for a watermark or the faint lines left by the papermaking screen.
- Try one print on damp paper and one on dry paper from the same block.

## Words to know

**Substrate:** the surface that receives the print.
**Sizing:** a coating or additive that makes paper less absorbent, so ink sits on top instead of soaking in.
**Grain:** the direction most of a paper's fibers run.
**Deckle:** the soft, uneven edge of handmade or mould-made paper.
