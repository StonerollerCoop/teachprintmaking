## 1. 4 Methods for At-Home Print Making

A four-page museum family activity sheet from spring 2020 (it asks families to tag `#MuseumFromHome`). It opens with Imna Arroyo's collagraph work as an example, then offers four methods. The first three link out to other sites; the fourth is written out in full.

**Common practice, no credit needed**

- Crayon transfer drawings: drawing on the back of a crayon-covered sheet to transfer the color.
- Shaving cream marbling with food coloring.
- Cardboard relief prints, including the reminder that letters must be reversed.
- Glass plate monotype with washable markers: draw on picture-frame glass, then press a lightly dampened sheet onto it with a hand, a wooden spoon, or a tennis ball. This is a lovely joy-collection item, since the plate and the press are both things already in the house.

**Particular, needs credit or care**

- The linked instructions for the first three methods belong to PBS Parents, First Palette, and Art Bar. Link to them or write our own; don't copy them.
- The Imna Arroyo example: if we use her as an artist reference, credit her by name and link to her work rather than reproducing the image, since her prints are in copyright.

**Fits:** planographic (glass plate monotype), relief (cardboard), joy collections (tennis ball and spoon as presses; glass, cardboard, and shaving cream as plates).

## 2. Techniques of printmaking

A 17-page illustrated overview of woodcut, wood engraving, linocut, engraving, etching, aquatint, sugar lift, mezzotint, drypoint, monotype, lithography, and screenprinting, with artist examples for each.

**Common practice, no credit needed**

- The definitions of every technique. These are general knowledge and appear in every printmaking text.

**Particular, needs credit or care**

- Much of the technical text closely follows Wikipedia, which is licensed CC BY-SA. We should write our own definitions rather than reuse these passages.
- The art-historical commentary appears to be the author's own: the reading of Rembrandt's *The Three Crosses*, the notes on Die Brücke, Blake's relief etching, and Warhol's screenprints. We can't reuse that writing, and we don't know who wrote it.
- The quoted tip about using coffee essence for sugar lift is someone's own words.
- Images: Dürer, Rembrandt, Hiroshige, Bewick, Goya, Castiglione, Lautrec, and Haeckel are all public domain, and museum open access copies exist. Picasso, Warhol, Henry Moore, Allen Jones, and James Dodds are in copyright; we can name them as artists to look at and link to museum pages, but not show their work.

**Fits:** overview pages for each family, and an "artists to look at" list per technique, drawn from this document's choices but written fresh.

## 3. A Student Guide to the Use of Soft Grounds in Intaglio Printmaking

Lori Jean Ash's 1984 MFA concentration paper from Colorado State University: a history of soft ground, then methods (recipes, drawing through tissue, pressing textures, stopping out, foul bite).

**Common practice, no credit needed**

- Soft ground as a technique, the standard recipe proportions (which she footnotes to earlier texts), drawing through tissue, and pressing fabric or paper into the ground.

**Particular, needs credit or care**

- Her teaching voice and judgments, such as choosing textures for what they do in the composition instead of for their own sake. If a lesson echoes her ideas, credit her as a reference.
- Figures by Lasansky and Hayter are in copyright.

**Safety note:** her methods use asphaltum, turpentine, and acid baths. A lesson built from this would be for adults in a ventilated studio, and it should point to the nontoxic acrylic soft ground in the Donna Adams compendium as the safer alternative.

## 4. The Beginners Compendium of Non-Toxic Intaglio Printmaking

Donna Adams's 61-page illustrated manual from her graduate certificate research at the Rochester Institute of Technology. Part One covers Intaglio-Type (ImagOn photopolymer film, more than thirty variations). Part Two covers metal salt etching with Edinburgh Etch and Saline Sulfate Etch and acrylic resists.

**Common practice, no credit needed**

- The basic idea of safer intaglio: photopolymer film instead of acid grounds, and metal salt etchants instead of nitric acid. These are now widely taught.

**Particular, needs credit or care**

- The whole document is © 2021. Its step sequences, timings, photos, and diagrams are hers.
- The methods have named originators, and the compendium itself credits Keith Howard for Intaglio-Type. Edinburgh Etch is generally credited to Friedhard Kiekeben, who runs the nontoxicprint site. Any lesson on these methods should name them.

**Fits:** intaglio, for college and adult classes. This is the strongest material we have for intaglio without toxic chemicals. I added Donna Adams to the outreach list, because permission to adapt even a few of her pages would fill much of the intaglio gap.

## 5. Intaglio printing: a chronology of papers used up to 1850

Ad Stijnman's article in *Papier Restaurierung* vol. 1 (2000), supplement, pages 91 to 110: a dated chronology of which papers plate printers used and why, from about 1430 to 1850, in English and German.

**Common practice:** historical facts themselves, such as early engravings being printed by rubbing before roller presses appeared, aren't owned by anyone.

**Particular:** the research and its arrangement are his. Cite him by name whenever we draw on it.

**Fits:** your point that printmaking makes students notice what paper they're using. This could anchor a short "about paper" page in the joy collections. It's a reference, not a lesson, so no permission is needed as long as we cite rather than copy.

## 6. Intaglio 101

A seven-page university studio handout marked "Maury 2015" covering drypoint, hard ground etching with ferric chloride, aquatint by rosin box and spray paint, printing, and roller care.

**Common practice, no credit needed**

- All of the procedures are standard studio practice: beveling plates, degreasing with whiting and ammonia, the blanket stack, wiping with tarlatan, and doubling exposure times for an aquatint test strip.

**Particular, needs credit or care**

- The wording, the blanket diagram, and the shop-specific rules. Write our own.

**Safety note:** this is a college shop handout. Ferric chloride, rosin dust, and hot plates call for adults, gloves, a respirator for rosin, and ventilation.

**Fits:** intaglio for college and adult classes, alongside the Donna Adams material.

## 7. Open Press Project Tips and Techniques

The 50-page ebook already listed in `open-sources.md`. It's CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, so we can link to it and share it unchanged, but we can't publish an adapted version.

**Common practice, no credit needed**

- Juice box (Tetra Pak) drypoint, CD drypoint, blind embossing, monotype with leaves, string, and onion netting, and dampening paper for intaglio. These are taught everywhere.

**Particular, needs credit or care**

- The 3D-printed relief and intaglio plates, which are their own development, and everything written for their specific press, including the troubleshooting pages.

**Fits:** joy collections (a press that fits in a backpack, plates from juice boxes and CDs) and intaglio for younger students.

## What this adds up to

- **Ready to write now, in our own words:** glass plate monotype, cardboard relief, juice box drypoint, blind embossing, and found-object monotype.
- **Needs an artist or source credit:** an Imna Arroyo collagraph reference, a soft ground lesson referencing Lori Jean Ash, and a paper history page citing Ad Stijnman.
- **Needs permission:** adapting Donna Adams's nontoxic intaglio methods.
- **Needs your help identifying the source:** 4 Methods, Techniques of printmaking, and Intaglio 101.
